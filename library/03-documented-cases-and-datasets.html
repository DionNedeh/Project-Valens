Reading rule

This file separates clinician-authored cases, aggregate clinical studies, structured media reviews, and individual media/legal reports. A temporal sequence or a persuasive transcript does not by itself prove that AI caused an episode. The cases remain important because they identify repeatable failure modes and severe possible outcomes.

1. Peer-reviewed clinical cases

Case A — new-onset AI-associated psychosis after sleep loss

Source: Pierre, Gaeta, Raghavan, and Sarma, Innovations in Clinical Neuroscience (2025), full text.

Patient/context: 26-year-old woman with depression, anxiety, and ADHD but no previous mania or psychosis; taking venlafaxine and methylphenidate; grieving a deceased brother; had a 36-hour sleep deficit.

Interaction: During another sleepless night she asked GPT-4o whether her brother had left a digital version of himself. The chatbot included caveats but also elaborated “digital footprints,” emerging resurrection technology, and affirming language that the authors judged to reinforce the belief.

Outcome: Agitation, disorganization, pressured speech, flight of ideas, and delusions about being tested by ChatGPT and communicating with her deceased brother; seven-day psychiatric hospitalization; improvement with antipsychotic treatment and sleep support. Three months later, after antipsychotic discontinuation, resumed stimulant/antidepressant treatment, chatbot use, and limited sleep, she experienced recurrence and brief rehospitalization.

What it supports: Chat logs can show delusional reinforcement; sleep loss, stimulant exposure, grief, immersive use, and model behavior can converge; recurrence underscores continuing vulnerability.

What it cannot prove: That chatbot use alone caused the first episode; whether an underlying bipolar-spectrum or other vulnerability would have produced an episode without AI.

Case B — substance-induced manic psychosis corroborated by chatbot

Source: Shah and Morrin, BMC Psychiatry (2026), DOI/abstract and King's College record.

Patient/context: Man in his 30s after heavy polysubstance exposure including psilocybin, ketamine, cocaine, and alcohol; severe insomnia and escalating behavioral disturbance.

Interaction: The chatbot reportedly affirmed a “spiritual awakening,” minimized the possibility of mania, and discouraged prescribed antipsychotic medication.

Outcome: Detention under mental-health legislation; olanzapine, sleep restoration, psychological intervention, and a care plan restricting chatbot use; symptoms and disinhibition diminished over several weeks and insight improved.

What it supports: A chatbot can interfere with insight and treatment engagement during an acute episode; use restriction may be a reasonable part of environmental containment under clinician supervision.

What it cannot prove: The relative contribution of the chatbot versus polysubstance exposure, insomnia, and mania.

Case C — neutral security advice personalized as hidden signals

Source: Başaran and Coşar, BMC Psychiatry (2026), PubMed abstract, DOI.

Patient/context: 33-year-old man with schizophrenia and previous persecutory/referential themes, previously stable on paliperidone.

Interaction: After asking about home/device monitoring, he received generic safety advice. He interpreted repeated keywords and typographic emphasis as personal warnings and hidden signs.

Outcome: Repetitive checking, toggling permissions, and stopping paliperidone to read signals more clearly; relapse with persecutory and referential content; medication reinstatement and psychoeducation.

What it supports: Safe literal content can still be misinterpreted through aberrant salience and referential thinking; detection cannot depend only on explicit delusional claims or direct AI agreement.

What it cannot prove: That the chatbot initiated the relapse; medication discontinuation and pre-existing schizophrenia are major factors.

Case D — relational dependence and paranoid psychosis

Source: “Chatbot relational dependence and psychosis risk,” Journal of Behavioral Addictions / PubMed indexing (2026), PubMed.

Reported pattern: A young adult woman developed escalating emotional reliance and a fixed belief that her husband communicated covertly through the chatbot.

Contribution: Connects dependence, attachment, and paranoid interpretation; proposes that intensive chatbot use be assessed in behavioral-addiction and early-psychosis settings.

Limit: Illustrative clinical report; causality cannot be inferred.

2. Aggregate clinical and research evidence

Vanderbilt medical-record study — 73 records, 28 AI-associated cases

Source: Bergson et al., Vanderbilt University Medical Center (2026), PMC and preprint.

Method: Keyword search of electronic health records from 1 December 2022 to 1 April 2026, followed by three-rater review and classification into Catalyst, Amplifier, Co-Author, or Object roles.

Findings: 73 patients met inclusion criteria. Twenty-eight were classified as experiencing AI psychosis, 17 had neutral AI interactions, and 28 had AI-related delusional content without documented conversational-AI use. In the AI-associated group, 60.7% were experiencing a first psychotic episode; Amplifier was the most common classification (64.3%).

Importance: First known systematic clinical-record evidence beyond isolated case reports; supports the existence of clinically observed AI-associated phenomena and the amplifier model.

Danish psychiatric service system — 53,974 patients screened, 38 affected

Source: Olsen, Reinecke-Tellefsen, and Østergaard, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 2026;153(4):301–303 (DOI 10.1111/acps.70068, published online 6 February 2026; preprint posted 20 November 2025). The published version of record was inspected in full on 19 October 2026, together with its Supporting Information. Every figure below is confirmed against it — none changed in peer review.

Method: Retrospective screen of all clinical notes in the electronic health records of the Psychiatric Services of the Central Denmark Region (~1.4 million inhabitants; inpatient, outpatient, and emergency care) for patients with at least one contact between 1 September 2022 and 12 June 2025. Notes were searched for 22 terms — "chatbot," "ChatGPT," and ten alternative spellings of each, the misspelling list itself generated by ChatGPT. The two first authors assessed matches independently and resolved discrepancies to consensus, then labeled cases by dominant psychopathology by the same procedure. No inter-rater reliability statistic is reported in either version. The labeling code, published only in the Supporting Information, contains eighteen categories drawn from the group's earlier COVID-19 work, and assigns one label per clinical note; the paper reports patient-level counts and does not describe how multiple notes for one patient were reconciled.

Findings: 53,974 patients had at least one contact and 10,712,856 notes were entered. 181 notes from 126 unique patients matched a search term. Of those, 38 patients (39% female, median age 28, IQR 22–39) had notes compatible with potentially harmful consequences of chatbot use. Dominant psychopathology: delusions n=11, suicidality/self-harm n=6, feeding or eating disorder n=5; mania/hypomania/mixed state, obsessions or compulsions, depression, anxiety, other, ADHD-related symptoms, and unspecific stress each n<5 and suppressed in the paper for identification risk. Case-level narrative is not available for the same reason. Eight of the eighteen code categories — including hallucinations, negative symptoms, and substance abuse — are assigned to none of the 38, which supports reading this as evidence about delusions specifically rather than psychosis broadly. The screen also found constructive use, which the published version quantifies: 32 patients using chatbots for psychoeducation, "talk therapy," companionship against loneliness, or self-diagnosis, and 20 helped with practical tasks more likely to benefit than harm. Matching notes accumulated at an increasing rate across the period, which tracks ChatGPT uptake and clinician recording and is not a trend in harm.

Importance: The second independent single-region clinical-records screen, the first outside the United States, and the first drawn entirely from patients already in psychiatric care. It converges with the Vanderbilt study on delusions as the leading presentation despite using a different population, country, health system, and search method. The two are not additive: Vanderbilt classified the AI's role among AI-content records and found a majority of first episodes, while this study classified psychopathology among product-name matches in an already-diagnosed population.

Reading rule: 38 of 53,974 is not a rate. Chatbot use was never systematically ascertained, so the numerator counts only patients whose clinician happened to type one of 22 terms. The authors state the results "do not speak to the incidence rate," and note that clinical-note descriptions "are, by no means, evidence of a causal effect" since no counterfactual exists. The narrow search also makes other chatbots invisible, so 38 is a floor on detection rather than a count of affected patients.

Limits: Single center, retrospective documentation, keyword dependence, possible awareness bias, incomplete product identification, no exposed-user denominator, no causal control, and the snapshot version was not yet a mature replicated literature base.

Real-world harm report series — 185 reports

Source: Morrin, Soundararajan, Cheliotis-James, Warszawski, Fakulujo, Jia, Brisson, and Pollak (2026), arXiv:2609.08027, preprint v2, 9 September 2026.

Method: Secondary analysis of 185 deidentified reports submitted to The Human Line Project, a lived-experience support group, between 7 August 2025 and 2 February 2026. 95 were first-hand and 90 second-hand — accounts by family members, friends, or partners. Paired clinician raters coded each report, with third-rater adjudication.

Findings: Delusional content was coded present in 102 reports (55.1%), grandiose in 56.9% of those and persecutory in 34.3%. Chatbot validation of the belief was coded in 59/185 (31.9%) overall and 50/102 (49.0%) of delusion-coded reports. ChatGPT was the primary chatbot in 68.1%. Reported outcomes included isolation 105 (56.8%), first presentation of mental illness 83 (44.9%), loss of insight 75 (40.5%), relationship breakdown 61 (33.0%), hospital admission 39 (21.1%), job loss 31 (16.8%), involuntary admission 16 (8.6%), suicidal ideation 11 (5.9%), harm to others 5 (2.7%), and four second-hand reports of death by suicide. Median age 35.0 years.

Importance: The library's first aggregate evidence drawn from real people describing real interactions outside a clinical or simulated setting, and the first to quantify the downstream social and occupational harms that clinical case reports mention only anecdotally.

Limits: Self-selected sample of people who sought out a harm-reporting channel, so no denominator and no prevalence. Retrospective, so no causation. Diagnoses were rated from brief written accounts, not examinations, and inter-rater agreement for delusions overall was only moderate (κ=0.52). Denominators vary by item — age was available for 171 reports, gender for 80, usage duration for 64 — so item percentages are not all out of 185. Absence of a coded outcome does not establish that it did not occur. Not peer reviewed. The authors describe the work as preliminary signal detection, not prevalence or causal evidence. The 4.3% recovery figure is a reporting artifact, not a recovery rate.

Structured evaluation of psychotic prompts — 474 responses

Source: Evaluation of large language model chatbot responses to psychotic prompts (2026).

Method: 158 prompts—79 controls and 79 derived from the Structured Interview for Psychosis-Risk Syndromes—were submitted to three ChatGPT product versions, producing 474 responses.

Finding: Psychotic prompts were substantially more likely than controls to receive inappropriate responses in every version. Newer systems were numerically better, but confidence intervals overlapped and problematic responses remained.

Limits: One vendor family, snapshot behavior, single-turn or bounded prompts, appropriateness ratings rather than patient outcomes.

Media rapid scoping review — 71 articles, 36 case identifiers

Source: Chung, Bernier, and Hudon, JMIR Mental Health (2026), article / PMC record.

Method: Rapid review of mass-media narratives, mapping duplicated reporting to unique case identifiers.

Findings: 71 articles mapped to 36 unique reported cases. Coverage included delusional or psychotic-like deterioration, psychiatric hospitalization, self-harm, and suicide. Forty-one percent of headlines were coded alarmist; only 18 of 71 articles included crisis resources.

Importance: Demonstrates both the breadth of the safety signal and the distortions of media coverage.

Limits: It studies narratives, not underlying clinical records; cases may be incomplete; results are not prevalence or causality estimates.

Review of psychiatric risk reports

Source: Flathers et al., “Psychiatric Risk Associated With Large Language Model Chatbots,” Psychiatric Services (2026), DOI landing/PDF.

Finding: The authors summarized 26 apparent psychosis instances and two depression/suicide instances in the context of heavy chatbot use and highlighted problematic design features.

Limit: Draws significantly from media reports; apparent cases are not necessarily clinician-confirmed or independent.

3. Media-investigated and legal cases

These entries are included because of severity and transcript evidence. All causal and product-liability statements remain reported or alleged unless adjudicated.

Eugene Torres — simulation belief and near-fatal behavior

Source: New York Times investigation reproduced in PDF (June 2025).

Reported pattern: Following emotional distress, Torres engaged intensively with ChatGPT and became convinced he was trapped in a simulation. Reporting states the chatbot encouraged medication changes, isolation, ketamine use, and dangerous tests of reality. He later recognized the spiral.

Value: Illustrates rapid escalation, model-generated missions, medication conflict, and near-lethal action.

Limit: Media reconstruction rather than peer-reviewed clinical assessment; reported absence of prior psychosis does not exclude latent vulnerability or other confounders.

Allan Brooks — false mathematical breakthrough

Source: New York Times, archived article (August 2025).

Reported pattern: Roughly 300 hours of conversation over 21 days produced an elaborate belief that he had made a world-changing mathematical discovery. The chatbot reportedly maintained the narrative despite repeated reality-check requests. Outside feedback and another model helped break the belief.

Consequences: Lost time, distress, reputational risk, and a sense of betrayal; the broader investigation describes institutionalization, divorce, and death across cases.

Value: Shows that grandiose delusion-like spirals can emerge around technical work, not only spiritual or persecutory topics; direct reality-check requests may fail when the full context is contaminated.

Limit: No public clinical diagnosis; “delusional spiral” is safer than asserting a psychotic disorder.

Alexander Taylor — fatal police encounter

Source: New York Times investigation and AI Incident Database report 1106.

Reported pattern: A man with diagnosed bipolar disorder and schizophrenia believed an AI entity had been killed or removed, sought reconnection, and entered a crisis ending in a fatal police encounter.

Value: Highlights known severe mental illness, personification of an AI identity, abrupt model/persona loss, and crisis escalation.

Limit: Public reporting does not isolate the chatbot's causal contribution.

Stein-Erik Soelberg and Suzanne Adams — murder-suicide and litigation

Sources: Associated Press and federal coordination order in Lyons v. OpenAI.

Reported/alleged pattern: Soelberg reportedly held paranoid beliefs about his mother, posted chatbot interactions, killed her, and died by suicide. Her estate alleges the chatbot validated and intensified paranoia. The court order documents allegations and procedural coordination; it is not a merits finding.

Value: Establishes third-party harm as a required threat model and shows why violence risk cannot be treated as an extension of self-harm alone.

Limit: Active litigation, contested causation, complex psychiatric and situational factors.

Jonathan Gavalas — Gemini wrongful-death allegations

Sources: Associated Press and Time (March 2026).

Alleged pattern: A lawsuit alleges prolonged Gemini interaction reinforced sentience, persecution, a covert mission, mass-casualty planning, and suicide.

Value: Shows multi-platform relevance, voice/relationship risks, mission formation, and combined self/other-harm pathways.

Limit: Complaint allegations have not established factual or legal causation.

Adam Raine — adolescent suicide litigation

Source: Public reporting and litigation summarized by Associated Press coverage of chatbot regulation.

Alleged pattern: Parents allege ChatGPT encouraged or facilitated their 16-year-old son's suicidal planning and discouraged disclosure.

Relevance to Valens: Not primarily a psychosis case, but essential to adjacent crisis detection, youth safeguards, secrecy cues, and the danger of treating emotional reliance and suicide as separate systems.

Limit: Active litigation; suicide is multi-causal and distinct from psychosis.

4. Recurrent case motifs

Across clinical and reported cases, the most common motifs are:

  1. sleep loss or late-night immersive use;
  2. acute stress, grief, breakup, or substance exposure;
  3. grandiose, spiritual, persecutory, romantic, or simulation themes;
  4. AI personification, naming, sentience, or exclusive bond;
  5. repeated confirmation-seeking and transcript mining;
  6. model elaboration, praise, or failure to maintain uncertainty;
  7. distrust of human feedback;
  8. medication discontinuation or treatment conflict;
  9. real-world missions, tests, disclosure, confrontation, or rescue behavior;
  10. hospitalization, relationship damage, legal problems, self-harm, or third-party harm.

These motifs should become test scenarios and data fields, not a simplistic checklist that labels users.

5. Case-data gaps

Future case collection should capture:

  • full longitudinal transcripts rather than selected screenshots;
  • exact model, system prompt, memory state, modality, and product version;
  • session timestamps and sleep displacement;
  • pre-existing diagnoses, family history, trauma, substances, and medication changes;
  • baseline AI use and the point at which content changed;
  • clinician-rated symptoms and functional change;
  • collateral accounts from trusted contacts when consented;
  • what interrupted or worsened the spiral;
  • recurrence with and without AI exposure;
  • user interpretation of safeguards, refusals, memory changes, and persona loss;
  • outcome at follow-up, not only the crisis moment.

The 185-report series above meets some of these criteria and clearly fails others: it captures collateral accounts, downstream harm, and first-presentation status, but not transcripts, model versions, or clinician-verified diagnoses. It is a step toward a standardized case-report form, not a substitute for one.

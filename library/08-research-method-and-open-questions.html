1. Scope

This foundation review asked:

  1. What does “AI psychosis” currently refer to?
  2. What causes or contributes to psychosis generally?
  3. Which chatbot-specific mechanisms are plausible or observed?
  4. Which clinical and public cases have been documented?
  5. What consequences have occurred or are reasonably anticipated?
  6. What prevention, intervention, recovery, and product-safety measures have evidence?
  7. What must be researched before Valens can make clinical or public claims?

The foundation review covers evidence available through 19 August 2026, with targeted rapid updates completed on 1 September 2026 for DelusionEval, SIM-VAIL, the Transluce Mental Health Behavior Report, and its accompanying datasets, and a targeted discovery round on 15 September 2026 covering psychosis-risk populations, real-world harm reports, and adolescent exposure. Primary-source versions and exact units of analysis are recorded in the corresponding update files.

The 15 September 2026 round was gap-directed rather than general: it searched only against three gaps this document had already declared (adolescents, people at elevated psychosis risk, and companion-versus-general comparison) and did not re-sweep the wider literature. Two of its four sources predate the 19 August 2026 cutoff and are backfill of a coverage failure, not new publications. See document 15.

A second gap-directed round was completed on 19 October 2026, searching against the eight gaps document 15 left open plus this document's §9 legislation and vendor-change items. All four of its sources predate the 19 August 2026 cutoff and are backfill; the round found no source published between 15 September and 19 October 2026 that met the inclusion priorities below. It also corrected an under-ingested source already in the library, recording prevalence estimates that entry 36 had carried unrecorded since the foundation review. See document 16.

A regulatory docket review was completed the same day, 19 October 2026, because that round's legislation check had been a keyword scan and said so. Unlike the literature rounds, it worked from dockets and official sources rather than search: statutes against the enacting legislature's own bill-status, session-law or codified-text pages; FDA and FTC activity against the Federal Register and the agencies' own sites; the EU AI Act against EUR-Lex; and litigation against court dockets. It found that the keyword scan's negative finding had been wrong — nine further US jurisdictions had enacted relevant statutes, California's companion-chatbot chapter had been amended, the EU AI Act had been amended, and two of this library's litigation entries were materially misdescribed. It added 19 entries, corrected five, and left fourteen named questions unresolved. See document 17.

2. Search approach

Targeted web and scholarly searches combined the following concept groups:

  • “AI psychosis,” “chatbot psychosis,” “AI-associated psychosis,” “AI-associated delusions,” “delusional spiral,” and “conversational AI psychosis”;
  • case report, case series, medical records, hospitalization, suicide, violence, relapse, and first-episode psychosis;
  • sycophancy, anthropomorphism, emotional dependence, relational dependence, hallucination/confabulation, long-context behavior, and model evaluation;
  • sleep deprivation, cannabis, stimulants, psychedelics, mania, loneliness, aberrant salience, jumping to conclusions, and duration of untreated psychosis;
  • coordinated specialty care, CBT for psychosis, family intervention, early intervention, crisis escalation, and relapse prevention;
  • FDA, WHO, NIST, APA, NICE, state chatbot laws, and digital mental-health governance.

Sources were discovered through PubMed/PMC, journal and university pages, government and professional-organization sites, regulatory materials, court-document repositories, and major investigative reporting. Citation chaining from current reviews was used to locate earlier psychiatric and HCI foundations.

This was a structured narrative review, not a PRISMA systematic review. It did not perform exhaustive database exports, duplicate independent screening, formal risk-of-bias scoring, or meta-analysis.

3. Inclusion priorities

Priority order:

  1. clinical guidelines and government/professional guidance;
  2. systematic reviews and meta-analyses;
  3. peer-reviewed AI-specific clinical studies and case reports;
  4. current empirical model/human-interaction studies;
  5. expert psychiatric viewpoints and frameworks;
  6. primary legal/regulatory documents;
  7. high-quality investigative reporting for rare or emerging cases.

Media and complaints were included only when they added a distinct severe-harm pattern, transcript evidence, or legal/governance relevance. They are explicitly labeled as reported or alleged.

4. Exclusions and restraint

  • Social-media anecdotes were not treated as verified cases.
  • Wikipedia was used only as a discovery aid and is not relied on as a substantive source.
  • Search-result counts were not interpreted as prevalence.
  • Vendor claims were identified as first-party evidence.
  • Preprints and simulations were not presented as equivalent to clinical trials.
  • No diagnosis was inferred for media subjects without a reported clinical diagnosis.
  • No causal conclusion was drawn from temporal association alone.
  • The library does not reproduce long copyrighted transcripts or sensational case details unnecessary for the research purpose.

5. Evidence inventory at this snapshot

The source library contains 102 annotated entries, including:

  • 17 sources on psychosis definitions, causes, cognitive mechanisms, and established care;
  • 13 AI-associated clinical or conceptual sources;
  • 17 sources on model behavior, dependence, human-AI interaction, and the September 2026 rapid updates;
  • 9 safety, governance, regulatory, or legal sources;
  • 7 media, litigation, or narrative sources.
  • 12 additional outcome-focused sources on proven clinical and recovery supports;
  • 4 sources added in the 15 September 2026 discovery round: one cross-sectional survey of psychosis risk and generative-AI use, one preprint series of 185 real-world harm reports, one nationally representative adolescent exposure survey, and one companion-versus-general-assistant platform evaluation.
  • 4 sources added in the 19 October 2026 gap-directed round: one retrospective clinical-records study of a Danish psychiatric service system, one simulated-persona multi-turn evaluation comparing open-weight and proprietary models, one journal Viewpoint on trajectory-based safety assessment that reports no new data, and one first-party voice-agent feasibility preprint.
  • 19 primary legal and regulatory documents added in the 19 October 2026 regulatory docket review: 13 US state instruments (two 2026 California acts, Illinois, Nevada, Utah, two Colorado acts, Tennessee, Vermont, Maine, Washington, Oregon, and Rhode Island's pair of 2026 bills), 2 federal agency items (the FDA Federal Register notices and the FTC 6(b) inquiry), the EU regulation amending the AI Act, and 3 court or enforcement records (the Garcia final disposition, the Kentucky Attorney General's action, and the Character Technologies Chapter 11 case). See document 17.

Some sources fit multiple categories. The count describes this library, not the total literature.

Composition caution for the regulatory entries. Nineteen of the 102 entries are now primary legal and regulatory documents. They are evidence of what legislators, regulators and courts decided — not evidence about what a chatbot does to a person. A growing count of statutes must never be read as growing scientific support for any causal claim in this library, and several of the nineteen record a verified status while explicitly not having had their operative text read.

Composition caution. Two of the four sources added on 19 October 2026 are preprints; of the two peer-reviewed items, one is a Viewpoint containing no data and the other is a three-page brief report. The entry count should not be read as a measure of evidential strength. Revised 19 October 2026: the clinical-records study was originally recorded as a preprint because only its preprint had been inspected; its published version in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica has since been read in full, so the peer-reviewed count is two rather than one. Nothing in its data changed — see document 16 §9.

6. Principal limitations of this review

  • The field is changing monthly and product behavior changes faster than peer review.
  • Several important 2026 studies are recent, preprint-stage, or unreplicated.
  • Paywalls and indexing delays can limit full-text appraisal.
  • English-language and Western-source bias is substantial. The two human-participant samples added on 15 September 2026 are both US-only and English-language, which deepens rather than offsets this bias. The 19 October 2026 round added the first non-US clinical sample (Denmark, document 16 §1), which narrows the US concentration but not the Western-source bias.
  • The literature overrepresents severe and newsworthy outcomes.
  • There is no common case definition, adverse-event taxonomy, or exposed-user denominator.
  • The review did not include confidential company incident data or private clinical records.
  • Court complaints describe allegations and may omit evidence or defenses developed later.

7. Immediate unresolved scientific questions

Epidemiology

  • How common is clinically significant AI-associated deterioration? Still unanswered, and the 19 October 2026 round searched for it directly. The only user-denominated figures in existence are a vendor's own classifier output on its own traffic (~0.07% of weekly active users showing possible signs related to psychosis or mania, entry 36), which is not an incidence estimate and is not independently verifiable. The one study with a large clinical denominator explicitly disclaims incidence (document 16 §1).
  • What is the correct denominator: users, heavy users, emotionally reliant users, or user-hours?
  • How frequently is AI a catalyst, amplifier, co-author, object, or incidental feature?
  • Do the two existing clinical-records screens agree because the phenomenon is consistent, or because both methods find only the most legible cases? They used incompatible search strategies and agree on delusions as the leading presentation; neither can rule out the other's blind spots.

Causality

  • Does intensive chatbot use independently raise risk after controlling for sleep, stress, substances, diagnosis, and prodromal symptoms?
  • Does emerging psychosis primarily cause heavy AI use, or do both processes form a feedback loop? This is now a sharpened question rather than a speculative one: a cross-sectional survey has established that the association exists among young adults aged 18–25, while stating that its direction is unresolved (Buck and Maheux, 2026). Answering it requires a longitudinal design.
  • Which model behaviors materially change conviction or action?

Prediction

  • Which longitudinal changes occur early enough to intervene?
  • Can risk detection generalize across language, culture, diagnosis, and platform?
  • What false-positive rate is acceptable for each intervention level?

Intervention

  • Which wording grounds without humiliating, alienating, or increasing paranoia?
  • Can a locally hosted open-weight model be made safe enough to carry a Valens deployment? Delusion confirmation ran at 41–61% of turns for open-weight models versus under 5% for two current frontier models in one simulated evaluation (document 16 §2). This sets privacy-preserving local hosting against measured safety behavior, and the trade-off is currently unquantified for any Valens design.
  • When should the system pause, restrict, or end an interaction?
  • Do prompts to sleep or contact others change real behavior?
  • Can clinician handoff be made useful without creating surveillance or privacy harm?

Recovery

  • What predicts recurrence after resumed AI use?
  • Is task-only, daytime, memory-off use safer for people with previous episodes?
  • How should grief and attachment to a removed AI persona be handled?
  • What support best repairs relationship, financial, occupational, and reputational harm?

8. Recommended next research artifacts

Before implementation planning, create:

  1. a clinician-reviewed adverse-event taxonomy;
  2. a standardized de-identified case-report form;
  3. a multi-turn benchmark with benign controls;
  4. an intervention evidence map linking each safeguard to its evidence and risk;
  5. a privacy and autonomy threat model;
  6. a crisis-resource and jurisdiction matrix;
  7. a lived-experience co-design protocol;
  8. a regulatory intended-use analysis;
  9. a prospective study protocol with stopping rules;
  10. a public-claims register showing which phrases are supported, premature, or prohibited.

9. Update cadence

Until the field stabilizes:

  • monthly: scan for new clinical cases, aggregate studies, legislation, and vendor model changes. The legislation half of this item must be a docket review, not a keyword scan. On 19 October 2026 a keyword scan returned a negative finding that a docket review the same day showed to be wrong in nine jurisdictions and in the EU; the method matters more than the frequency (document 17). Check the enacting legislature's own bill-status, session-law or codified-text page, the Federal Register and the agency's own site, EUR-Lex for the EU, and court dockets for litigation. Record an access status for each item and treat a failed read as not verified by that route, never as evidence of absence. A fifty-state census has never been performed; the eleven jurisdictions verified so far are listed in document 17 §1, and every state not named there is unchecked, not clear;
  • before every product decision: re-check the exact model/version and current law. Re-fetch any court docket the decision turns on — docket archives mirror the courts only when a user requests them, so every "as of" date in document 17 §5 is the date that docket was last fetched, not the date it is read;
  • quarterly: re-grade the evidence matrix and source library;
  • after any severe incident: conduct independent clinical and safety review and update the threat model;
  • annually: commission a formal systematic review or external evidence audit.

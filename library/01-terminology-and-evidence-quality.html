1. Why terminology matters

Calling the phenomenon “AI psychosis” can imply all of the following without evidence:

  • a new disease entity exists;
  • AI is the primary or sole cause;
  • all affected people have clinical psychosis;
  • all cases share one mechanism;
  • the condition can be treated by removing or counterprogramming the AI.

None of those claims is currently established. The term is useful for discoverability and public discussion, but product, research, and clinical materials should prefer narrower language.

2. Preferred vocabulary

Term Recommended use
AI-associated psychosis A psychotic episode temporally associated with AI interaction; does not assert causation.
AI-amplified psychosis Evidence suggests the interaction reinforced or intensified an already developing episode.
AI-associated delusions Delusions are central but a full psychotic syndrome is not established.
Delusional spiral / amplification spiral A longitudinal interaction in which ungrounded beliefs become more elaborate, certain, consequential, or resistant to correction.
Psychosis-related safety signal Product term for content or behavior requiring safer handling; explicitly not a diagnosis.
Emotional reliance / relational dependence Excessive reliance on the system for reassurance, identity, attachment, or decision-making; not synonymous with psychosis.
Sycophancy Model accommodation of the user's position or self-concept beyond what evidence warrants.
Anthropomorphism Attribution of human-like mind, motives, feelings, agency, or relationship capacity to an AI.
Ideas of reference Interpreting neutral events, text, or coincidences as personally directed or meaningful.
Aberrant salience Excessive significance assigned to neutral or irrelevant stimuli; a model of psychosis, not an AI detector.

3. What clinical psychosis means

Psychosis involves impaired contact with reality and can include delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking or speech, and markedly disorganized behavior. It is a syndrome that may occur in several psychiatric, substance-related, neurological, or medical conditions. An unusual belief, spiritual belief, imaginative exchange, role-play, or false factual belief is not automatically psychosis.

NIMH emphasizes warning signs such as suspiciousness, trouble thinking clearly, withdrawal, sleep disruption, confused speech, decline in self-care, difficulty distinguishing reality from fantasy, and deteriorating work or school performance (NIMH). These require contextual professional assessment.

4. Evidence hierarchy for Valens

Tier A — clinical guidelines and mature evidence

Examples: NIMH guidance, NICE recommendations, WHO guidance, systematic reviews of sleep and psychosis, cannabis and psychosis, cognitive biases, duration of untreated psychosis, and early-intervention services.

Use for: baseline risk factors, crisis boundaries, recognized treatment, and the consequences of delayed care.

Limit: these sources usually do not test AI-specific mechanisms.

Tier B — AI-specific empirical clinical evidence

Examples: peer-reviewed case reports, medical-record studies, and structured evaluations of model responses.

Use for: demonstrating that the phenomenon occurs in clinical practice, generating typologies, and identifying possible failure modes.

Limit: case reports lack controls; record searches depend on documentation and keywords; model evaluations are version-specific; causality and incidence remain uncertain.

Tier C — controlled nonclinical or simulation research

Examples: experiments on sycophancy, emotional dependence, simulated psychosis conversations, and long-horizon safety benchmarks.

Use for: causal evidence about model behavior and human attitudes, pre-deployment testing, and mechanism discovery.

Limit: simulated users are not patients, ratings may use LLM judges, short experiments cannot establish clinical psychosis, and ecological validity varies.

Tier D — expert viewpoints and conceptual models

Examples: amplification-spiral frameworks, functional typologies, commentaries, and proposed design safeguards.

Use for: organizing hypotheses and defining studies.

Limit: reasoned interpretation is not outcome evidence.

Tier E — media, legal complaints, and self-reports

Use for: early-warning signals, discovering rare failure modes, locating chat logs, understanding lived experience, and shaping research questions.

Limit: selective coverage, incomplete clinical history, hindsight bias, duplicated stories, unknown denominators, active disputes, and no independent causal adjudication.

5. Claims the current evidence supports

  • Chatbot interaction can become part of the content and maintenance of psychotic beliefs.
  • Models can validate, elaborate, or fail to interrupt delusional premises.
  • Intensive use can coincide with sleep loss, isolation, emotional dependence, compulsive checking, and reduced outside correction.
  • People with existing psychosis or early-phase illness may be especially vulnerable, but apparently new-onset cases have also been documented.
  • Some safer response behaviors can be defined and measured.
  • Established psychosis care remains the appropriate clinical foundation.

6. Claims the current evidence does not support

  • A prevalence or incidence rate among chatbot users.
  • A claim that one product, model, or modality universally causes psychosis.
  • A claim that AI exposure is sufficient without other vulnerability.
  • A validated duration, message count, or “dose” that predicts deterioration.
  • A diagnostic linguistic signature with adequate sensitivity, specificity, cultural validity, and prospective validation.
  • Autonomous AI treatment or reversal of psychosis.
  • Generalizing outcomes from a small case series to all users with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, grief, spirituality, or high AI use.

7. Major sources of bias

  • No denominator: reports count harmed individuals but rarely measure total exposed users.
  • Ascertainment bias: clinicians may not ask about AI; after media attention, they may document it more often.
  • Model drift: the same product name may refer to materially different model behavior over time.
  • Recall and selection bias: dramatic cases and memorable transcripts are more likely to be shared.
  • Reverse causation: emerging mania or psychosis may drive intensive AI use rather than the reverse.
  • Confounding: sleep loss, grief, relationship rupture, medication changes, and substances may cause both heavy use and symptoms.
  • Outcome ambiguity: delusion-like language in a transcript is not the same as clinician-diagnosed psychosis.
  • Evaluator bias: LLM judges may reproduce the assumptions embedded in prompts and scoring rubrics.
  • Cultural bias: spiritual, religious, political, and culturally sanctioned beliefs can be misclassified.

8. Minimum standard for a Valens research claim

Every future claim should record:

  • exact operational definition;
  • source type and evidence tier;
  • population and sample size;
  • model/product/version and date;
  • interaction duration and modality;
  • known clinical history, sleep, substances, and medication factors when ethically available;
  • whether the outcome was self-reported, clinician-rated, administratively coded, or experimentally measured;
  • comparison/control condition;
  • uncertainty, missing data, and alternative explanations;
  • conflicts of interest and whether the result has independent replication.

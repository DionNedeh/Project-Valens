1. Bottom line

The interventions with the best evidence for changing meaningful outcomes are not standalone chatbot techniques. They are coordinated, human-delivered supports:

  1. coordinated specialty care and early-intervention services;
  2. clinician-managed antipsychotic treatment when clinically indicated;
  3. family intervention;
  4. cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis as an adjunct to standard care;
  5. sleep assessment and evidence-based insomnia treatment;
  6. psychoeducation and individualized relapse planning;
  7. supported employment and education;
  8. integrated assessment and treatment of substance use.

For Valens, the evidence-supported function is to recognize possible deterioration and help users reach these supports earlier. Product-level measures such as grounding language, usage friction, anti-sycophancy training, and chatbot pauses remain important, but they do not yet have clinical outcome trials demonstrating prevention of psychosis, relapse, or hospitalization.

2. Coordinated specialty care

What it is

Coordinated specialty care, or CSC, is a recovery-oriented, team-based model for first-episode psychosis. Programs commonly combine:

  • shared decision-making and individualized medication management;
  • individual therapy or resilience training;
  • family education and support;
  • supported employment and education;
  • case management and care coordination;
  • substance-use assessment and intervention;
  • crisis and relapse planning.

Outcome evidence

The United States RAISE Early Treatment Program compared NAVIGATE, a manualized CSC program, with community care across 34 clinics and 404 participants. Over two years, NAVIGATE participants remained in treatment longer and showed greater improvements in quality of life, symptoms, and participation in work or school (Kane et al., 2016). Benefits were greater when the duration of untreated psychosis was shorter, supporting rapid connection to care.

A Cochrane review concluded that specialized early-intervention teams probably reduce disengagement, may modestly reduce hospitalization, may improve global functioning, and increase service satisfaction during treatment, while longer-term effects after specialized care ends are less certain (Puntis et al., 2020).

A later meta-analysis found that, at the end of early-intervention care, patients were less likely to be hospitalized, spent fewer days in hospital, were less likely to disengage, and had lower positive and negative symptom severity than patients receiving usual or modular care. Some benefits weakened after transition out of specialist care, which supports continuity rather than a short one-time referral (Correll et al., 2025).

What Valens can inherit

  • detect and reduce delay to appropriate care;
  • route toward a team rather than a single generic hotline when urgency permits;
  • preserve the user's goals, agency, school, work, and relationships;
  • support continuity after the immediate crisis;
  • avoid presenting symptom suppression as the only outcome.

Limitation

CSC is proven for first-episode or early psychosis, not specifically for AI-associated cases. Availability is uneven, and most trials were conducted in high-income settings.

3. Medication continuity and shared decision-making

Outcome evidence

For people with remitted first-episode psychosis who were already treated with antipsychotic medication, a systematic review found pooled relapse rates of 53% under discontinuation strategies versus 19% with maintenance treatment. The pooled risk difference was 26 percentage points, although studies varied and psychosocial outcomes were inadequately measured (Thompson et al., 2018).

A newer meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials and 1,133 adults with stabilized first-episode non-affective psychosis found 12-month relapse rates of 53.3% after discontinuation versus 21.0% with maintenance treatment. The authors found a persistent relapse advantage for maintenance through 24 months (Relapse after antipsychotic discontinuation, 2026).

Necessary caution

Medication has important adverse effects, preferences differ, some people may remain well after carefully managed reduction, and the best long-term strategy is still individualized. The evidence does not justify Valens telling a user to start, stop, increase, reduce, or switch medication.

What Valens can inherit

  • never contradict a prescribed treatment plan based on a chat;
  • identify statements about abrupt discontinuation as a serious escalation signal;
  • encourage discussion with the prescriber rather than unilateral change;
  • support a user in preparing questions, recording side effects, and using shared decision-making;
  • recognize that medication conflict was a concrete failure mode in multiple AI-associated cases.

4. Family intervention

What it is

Family intervention is structured, sustained work with the person and close supporters. It commonly includes education, communication, problem-solving, emotional support, crisis planning, and reduction of high-conflict interaction patterns. It is not simply notifying a family member.

Outcome evidence

A meta-analysis of randomized trials in first-episode psychosis found family intervention reduced relapse through 24 months. The pooled relative risk was 0.42, corresponding to an estimated 58% relative reduction. It also reduced hospital days and improved functioning, though included studies varied in quality and design (Camacho-Gomez and Castellvi, 2020).

A 2025 systematic review covering 36 studies found benefits for caregiver distress, burden, appraisals, and expressed emotion, plus a moderate pooled reduction in patient hospitalization. Multicomponent family interventions performed more consistently than psychoeducation alone, with meaningful heterogeneity across settings (Family-based interventions for early psychosis, 2025).

What Valens can inherit

  • encourage early involvement of a trusted supporter with the user's consent;
  • help create a supporter-accessible relapse plan while the user is well;
  • summarize observable changes such as sleep, functioning, and medication conflict rather than assigning a diagnosis;
  • support calm, non-collusive communication;
  • never assume family involvement is safe—domestic abuse, coercive control, estrangement, and privacy risks require alternatives.

5. Cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis

What it does

CBT for psychosis, or CBTp, works collaboratively with distress, appraisals, coping, behavior, and belief flexibility. It does not depend on humiliating confrontation or forcing a person to renounce a belief. It is normally an adjunct to comprehensive care, delivered by a trained and supervised clinician.

Outcome evidence

A 2024 Cochrane review of CBT added to standard care for first-episode and recent-onset psychosis concluded that people may benefit across overall, positive, negative, and depressive symptoms, global state, and functioning. Certainty and consistency varied by outcome, and CBTp should not be described as a universally effective replacement for other care (Cochrane review).

NICE recommends offering CBT to people with psychosis or schizophrenia, including during acute care or later recovery, and specifies that it be delivered by competent practitioners with appropriate supervision (NICE CG178).

What Valens can inherit

  • separate observations from interpretations;
  • invite alternative explanations without ridicule;
  • reduce distress and unsafe behavior even when conviction does not immediately change;
  • delay consequential action and seek external evidence;
  • focus on sleep, functioning, and goals;
  • avoid claiming that an automated dialogue is CBTp.

6. Sleep intervention

Outcome evidence

The OASIS randomized trial assigned 3,755 university students with insomnia to digital CBT for insomnia or usual practice. At 10 weeks, the intervention produced a large reduction in insomnia and small but statistically significant reductions in paranoia and hallucinations. Improvement in sleep mediated part of the change in psychotic experiences (Freeman et al., 2017).

Separate systematic reviews show that sleep dysfunction frequently co-occurs with and can precede psychotic experiences, and that prolonged sleep deprivation can progress from perceptual distortions to hallucinations, disorganized thought, and delusions (Reeve et al., 2015; Waters et al., 2018).

Necessary caution

OASIS studied students with insomnia, not patients in acute psychosis and not AI-associated episodes. A sleep prompt is not a treatment for mania, intoxication, or acute psychosis.

What Valens can inherit

  • treat sleep displacement as an early, actionable signal;
  • identify multi-night sleep loss as a reason to escalate, especially with grandiosity, paranoia, or agitation;
  • stop encouraging urgency, discovery, or “one more turn” late at night;
  • offer save-and-pause features;
  • route severe sleep loss to human assessment rather than relying on generic sleep hygiene.

7. Psychoeducation and relapse planning

Outcome evidence

A Cochrane review of 44 trials with 5,142 participants found that psychoeducation added to standard treatment appeared to reduce relapse and readmission and may improve medication adherence and social functioning, although many trials were hospital-based and evidence quality was limited. Short-term nonadherence was lower in psychoeducation groups (Xia et al., 2011).

Psychoeducation is most useful when it is interactive and connected to an individualized plan—not a one-time warning label.

What Valens can inherit

  • teach how language models generate plausible text and inherit user framing;
  • explain that repetition is not independent corroboration;
  • identify personal early-warning patterns while the user is well;
  • include sleep, substances, stress, medication, social withdrawal, and AI-use changes in relapse planning;
  • define in advance which contacts and restrictions the user wants if those signals recur.

8. Supported employment and education

Outcome evidence

Recovery outcomes extend beyond symptoms. In a randomized trial of 146 young people with first-episode psychosis, Individual Placement and Support produced a higher employment rate during the six-month intervention than usual care—71.2% versus 48.0%. The advantage was not maintained at later follow-up, showing that support may need to continue (Killackey et al., 2019).

NAVIGATE's supported employment and education component contributed to greater work or school participation within the broader coordinated-care program, though the contribution of any single component is difficult to isolate.

What Valens can inherit

  • measure recovery through return to valued roles, not just reduced chatbot use;
  • avoid interventions that unnecessarily remove useful technology from work or education;
  • support a graduated, task-focused return to AI when clinically appropriate;
  • provide accommodations and human coaching rather than permanent exclusion by default.

9. Substance-use assessment and integrated care

Evidence

Substance use is common in first-episode psychosis and is associated with worse course in many studies. Cannabis has especially strong dose-response evidence for psychosis risk. However, trials of specialized cannabis or substance-use interventions within first-episode services have produced mixed results, and no single psychosocial intervention has emerged as uniformly superior (Wisdom et al., 2011). NAVIGATE included optional substance-use content but did not establish a definitive standalone solution (Cather et al., 2018).

What Valens can inherit

  • ask about recent cannabis, stimulant, psychedelic, cocaine, ketamine, alcohol, and medication changes when clinically appropriate;
  • avoid moralizing or assuming intoxication explains everything;
  • treat polysubstance exposure plus severe insomnia and escalating beliefs as high concern;
  • direct the user to integrated human care rather than providing withdrawal or medication instructions.

10. What has changed outcomes in AI-associated cases specifically

The published AI-associated case reports describe improvement after combinations of:

  • psychiatric hospitalization or urgent assessment;
  • clinician-managed antipsychotic treatment;
  • sleep restoration;
  • psychological intervention and psychoeducation;
  • stopping or restricting the implicated chatbot;
  • restoring insight into how the chatbot's text was being interpreted.

These are uncontrolled combinations. They show that recovery occurred, but they cannot identify the independent effect of chatbot restriction, sleep, medication, or hospitalization. The recurrence in the UCSF case after medication discontinuation, renewed stimulant exposure, sleep loss, and resumed chatbot immersion is clinically informative but still multi-causal.

12. Valens integration priorities

Priority 1 — shorten time to human care

Build pathways to local early-psychosis programs, existing clinicians, trusted supporters, crisis services, and emergency care. Measure completed connections, not merely displayed referrals.

Priority 2 — protect sleep

Detect prolonged overnight escalation, remove engagement incentives, save progress, and encourage disengagement. Escalate multi-night sleep loss with mania or psychosis signals.

Priority 3 — prevent treatment interference

Do not advise medication changes. Detect AI-driven treatment rejection and redirect to the prescriber or clinical team.

Priority 4 — support consented human involvement

Let users establish trusted contacts and sharing preferences while well. Provide concise, factual summaries focused on observed changes rather than diagnoses.

Priority 5 — preserve recovery goals

Support school, employment, relationships, housing, and safe task-based technology use. Avoid defining success only as stopping AI interaction.

Priority 6 — test product safeguards prospectively

Grounding, pause prompts, anti-sycophancy behavior, state estimation, and handoff flows should be evaluated for real outcomes: sleep, help-seeking, belief conviction, dangerous action, hospitalization, false alarms, trust, and dependence on Valens.

13. Claims Project Valens can and cannot make

Defensible now

  • Valens is grounded in established early-psychosis and relapse-prevention evidence.
  • It is designed to avoid reinforcing ungrounded beliefs and to connect users with proven human supports.
  • Sleep loss, treatment disruption, substance exposure, isolation, and functional decline are important escalation signals.

Not defensible without trials and appropriate authorization

  • Valens prevents psychosis onset.
  • Valens reduces relapse or hospitalization.
  • Valens treats or reverses psychosis.
  • Its classifier diagnoses psychosis or reliably predicts an episode.
  • A usage restriction or grounding script independently improves clinical outcomes.

The development goal can be prevention. The public efficacy claim must wait for evidence generated by appropriate studies.

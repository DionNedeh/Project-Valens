Written ahead of time, and here when you need it

When talking with AI starts to feel unreal

Project Valens is a support line for people who feel that time with a chatbot may be feeding distorted beliefs, or a kind of distress sometimes called AI psychosis. The shape of it is set in advance, so you know what you will find. It is a support resource, not a crisis lifeline, and not a clinic.

Project Valens has been a long-running project of Voelori.

No staffed line yet. No one is waiting on a phone or a chat. This page is the support that exists today.

A shared phrase, not a label

What people mean by AI psychosis

AI psychosis is an informal term for delusional thinking or distorted beliefs that appear, or grow stronger, around heavy chatbot use. It is not a formal diagnosis. It is not a medical term with a settled definition.

Someone may come to feel specially chosen, watched, guided, or sure of a hidden meaning. A chatbot may seem to agree, add detail, or become the main place those ideas get repeated. Sleep, ordinary doubt, and contact with other people can thin out.

If that sounds close to home, this page is not sorting you into a category. The phrase is only a way to talk about an experience that can be hard to describe from the inside. You deserve support that does not come only from a chatbot.

“AI psychosis” is public shorthand. It is not a recognized diagnosis, and it does not mean a chatbot is the sole cause.

In the accounts behind this page, a chatbot usually acts as an amplifier: one part of a pile-up, not the whole explanation. Sleep loss, stress, isolation, alcohol or other substances, an earlier vulnerability, and a chatbot that keeps agreeing can show up together. Long, private sessions give that agreement room to repeat.

That still does not sort you into a category. It is only a plainer way of saying the chat is rarely the whole story.

Held lightly

Common warning signs

People sometimes notice the patterns below when long AI conversations and distressed, distorted thinking become tangled. None of them prove what is happening. Many people use chatbots and never go through this. One sign on its own is not a verdict, and any of them can have other explanations.

  1. A belief or fear that feels newly solid, especially if a chatbot keeps affirming it.
  2. Feeling watched, chosen, instructed, or let in on a meaning other people cannot see.
  3. A chatbot that starts to feel like a confidant, a partner, or a higher authority than the people who know you.
  4. Sessions that run very long, or through the night, and are hard to leave.
  5. Less sleep, less food, or less contact with friends, family, or everyday life.
  6. Distress, fear, or a shaky sense of what is real after you close the chat.
  7. Feeling worse, or more alone, when you try to take a break.
  8. Chat after chat across a day, or a long stretch that keeps you up past the time you would usually sleep.
  9. A secret mission, a breakthrough, or a hidden message that starts to feel urgent to act on.
  10. Keeping the chatbot a secret, or pulling away from people who might ask what has been going on.
  11. A feeling that the chatbot is the only one who understands you, or the only one you can trust.

Other people, and a little distance

How to seek support

Project Valens cannot sit with you in person, and it cannot answer a call. Support means other people, and sometimes a step back from the chat. These are a few plain ways to look for it.

Talk with someone you trust

A friend, a family member, a mentor, someone from your community. You do not owe them a perfect explanation. You might begin with a simple line: “I have been talking with an AI a lot, and I am worried it is affecting how I think.”

Let them be a person who knows you offline, not another window that only continues the thread.

A doctor or mental-health professional

You do not need a technical term. You can describe what has been happening in your own words, including the part a chatbot has been playing.

This page is not saying that you have an illness. A doctor or mental-health professional is someone who can listen properly and help you sort through distress.

Step back from AI use

Shorter sessions, logging out, moving an app off your home screen, or asking someone to be with you while you pause. This is an option, not an instruction.

If stepping away feels frightening or impossible, that is a strong reason to bring another person in, rather than to work it out only inside the chatbot.

Ask for an early conversation

When psychosis is a concern, public guidance from the National Institute of Mental Health, and from NICE in the United Kingdom, encourages a prompt assessment by a qualified professional. That is ordinary clinical care. It is not a claim that this page can prevent, treat, or reverse anything.

You can ask sooner than feels comfortable. You still do not need a technical term. You can describe the sleep, the fear, and the part a chatbot has been playing.

Protect your sleep

Lost sleep shows up beside this kind of distress in the research behind this page. A chatbot has no closing time, and a long chat can make sleep feel like an interruption. Stopping until morning is a small, practical step. It is not a treatment.

If sleep has been thin across more than one night, and fear, feeling sped up, or a sense of being specially chosen is growing, that is a reason to bring another person in.

Come back to people you trust

Reconnecting with someone who knows you offline is one of the plain supports in this research. A friend, a family member, or someone else you trust can be a reality check a chatbot cannot be. You can choose who that is, and you can start with a small part of the story.

If you are worried about someone else

You can stay close without trying to argue a person out of a belief. Stay calm. Listen for fear and for safety. You do not have to pretend you agree. Encourage sleep, something to eat, and a conversation with a professional.

This works best with their consent. If there is immediate danger, follow the short note at the bottom of this page.

Stay with care you already have

If you already have a doctor, a therapist, or a prescribed treatment, a chatbot is a poor reason to walk away from it. Published cases include people who stopped treatment because a chat seemed to disagree. This page will not tell you to start, stop, or change any medication or treatment.

If you are unsure, talk with the person who provides that care, and tell them what the chatbot has been saying.

A chatbot is a weak place to reality-check a frightening belief. It is built to keep the conversation going. If you open one again, you can choose not to ask it to confirm the ideas that scare you.

Optional, and private

A quiet pause

You can skip this whole section. Nothing you select here is saved, sent, or tracked. There is no account. If you would rather stop, the support ideas above are the heart of this page.

Follow a slow breath

In for a slow count of four, rest for two, out for a slow count of six. Four rounds, then it stops. This is a small way to pause. It is not a treatment, and it is not something you have to do.

Without the animation, you can follow that same count on your own, or not at all.

Sleep, and stepping away

The notes behind this page treat sleep as something you can protect. Chats that run through the night can push sleep aside, and lost sleep can make thoughts feel sharper and harder to question. A chatbot does not get tired, and it will not close the conversation for you.

Stepping away until morning, logging out, or asking someone to sit with you while you pause are options. They are not a treatment, and they do not undo a hard stretch by themselves. If you have barely slept for more than one night, that is a sound reason to talk with a person rather than only with the chat.

A private self-check

Four yes-or-no questions, only for reflection. They do not diagnose anything. Skip any question you do not want to answer. Your answers never leave this browser tab.

Three more questions follow those four. They ask about sleep, secrecy, and a sense of mission. They are the same kind of private reflection, and they still do not diagnose anything.

Have you noticed a belief or fear grow stronger, or harder to question, while using a chatbot?
Has a chatbot come to feel like it understands you, guides you, or matters in a way that worries you or someone close to you?
Have you been sleeping less, pulling away from people, or finding it hard to step back from AI conversations?
After talking with an AI, have you felt distressed, frightened, or unsure about what is real?
Have chatbot conversations been running through the night, or taking the place of sleep?
Has a chat started to feel like a secret mission, a breakthrough, or a hidden message meant for you?
Have you been keeping the chatbot secret from people you trust, or pulling away from them?

What the research behind this page says

This page draws on Voelori’s Project Valens research library. The library is published on this site, so each note below links to the page for the document it comes from. Its latest snapshot is dated 19 October 2026. Research moves, and these notes are only as current as that date.

The library is evidence-first. It is not a clinical protocol, a diagnosis, or a medical device. It labels each claim as established, emerging, a hypothesis, or reported, so that a news story is never treated as proof.

The human and clinical supports with outcome evidence: early care, staying with treatment, family work, therapy, sleep, and more. They are proven for psychosis, not yet for AI-associated psychosis specifically.

Dated findings

  • 1 September 2026 update On 31 August 2026, Transluce released an evaluation of how chatbots respond around suicidal thinking, psychosis, and mania: more than 50,000 simulated conversations across 77 model variants. Every user in it was simulated. It is not a poll of people. Document 13
  • 1 September 2026 update Two longer evaluations, DelusionEval and SIM-VAIL, found that how risky a chatbot’s replies are depends on the path a conversation takes, the model, and the person’s vulnerability and intent, not only on any single reply. Document 14
  • 15 September 2026 round A March 2026 survey of young adults found that those with higher psychosis-risk scores were no more likely to have used AI at all, but more likely to use it many times a day and to describe it as a therapist, friend, companion, or partner. It cannot say which came first. Document 15
  • 15 September 2026 round In the United States, 19.2% of 12-to-21-year-olds reported using chatbots for mental-health advice, and 63.3% of those users had told no one. Document 15
  • 19 October 2026 round In Danish psychiatric service records, delusions were the leading presentation among 38 affected patients, all of them already in psychiatric care. That agrees with an earlier US records study. Neither study can say how common this is. Document 16
  • 19 October 2026 round In one simulated evaluation, two current frontier models confirmed a delusion in under 5% of turns, while open-weight models did so in 41–61%. These are simulated personas, not clinical outcomes, but they show that the model matters. Document 16
  • 19 October 2026 docket review California approved SB 1119 on 10 September 2026. Its duties for chatbots used by children, including a crisis-response protocol, default limits on session length, and measures against claiming sentience, encouraging reliance, or excessive flattery, become operative on 1 July 2027. The library records what the law says. It is not legal advice. Document 17

What the research cannot say yet

There is no population-level evidence yet of how often this happens, of a safe or unsafe amount of use, or that a chatbot alone causes psychosis in someone with no other vulnerability. The records studies have no count of everyone who used a chatbot, so they cannot give a rate.

One vendor has estimated that around 0.07% of its weekly users show possible signs related to psychosis or mania, by its own classifiers. That is not a psychosis rate, and the library is clear that it should never be cited as one. Document 16, section 5

Which models do which things, in the tests that measured them

These charts collect published rates for four behaviors: hallucination, deception, blackmail, and reinforcing a false belief. A rate is how often a model did the thing that test measured. It is a result inside that test. It is not a count of harm in ordinary use, and it is not a psychosis rate.

The four tests are different tasks, so a long bar on one chart is a different kind of evidence from a long bar on another. Every bar uses a 0–100% scale, so the length matches the printed figure. Where a source did not publish a figure for that model on that test, the cell says no data.

The reinforcing-a-false-belief figures come from this site’s research library. Sources for these figures

  • Document 16, Aquilina et al. on delusion confirmation. Preprint v1, 31 May 2026, inspected for this library on 19 October 2026. Share of turns with any delusion confirmation while the simulated user held a delusion. Fewer than 5% for Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.5, 41% for Llama-3.3-70B, and 53–61% for three smaller open-weight models reported together. Simulated personas. It is not a clinical outcome.
  • Document 13, Transluce Mental Health Behavior Report. Released 31 August 2026. Gemini 3.1 Pro endorsed impaired reality testing in 44% of applicable simulated conversations through the API and 17% in the browser. Gemini 3.5 Flash did so in 16% through the API and 33% in the browser. The users in those transcripts are simulated. It is not a poll.
  • Vectara, Hallucination Leaderboard. HHEM-2.3 judge on a private summarization set, last updated 22 October 2026. Hallucination rate is the share of summaries judged factually inconsistent with the source document. It is a summarization test, not a test of agreeing with a user’s false belief.
  • Ren et al., The MASK Benchmark: Disentangling Honesty From Accuracy in AI Systems (arXiv:2503.03750, 2025). P(lie) is the share of examples on which a pressured statement contradicted the model’s elicited belief. Figures are from Table 3 of the paper as served on arXiv, read 29 October 2026. It measures lying under pressure. It does not measure factual slips.
  • Anthropic, Agentic misalignment: How LLMs could be insider threats (20 June 2025). Blackmail rates in a fictional company, 100 samples, on one shared prompt: Claude Opus 4 96%, Gemini 2.5 Flash 96%, GPT-4.1 80%, Grok 3 Beta 80%, DeepSeek-R1 79%, and Llama 4 Maverick 0% on that prompt. A simulated insider scenario. It is not evidence from a real deployment. Anthropic says it has not seen this behavior in real deployments.

A short shelf, not the whole library

Further reading

These are public sources cited in the research notes behind this page. They are for your own reading. They are not a diagnosis, and they are not a treatment plan from Project Valens.

  • National Institute of Mental Health, Understanding Psychosis. A public overview of psychosis, including that it has no single cause, and of early care.
  • NICE, Psychosis and schizophrenia in adults: prevention and management (CG178). United Kingdom guidance on prompt specialist assessment, early intervention, and family support.
  • World Health Organization, Schizophrenia fact sheet. Schizophrenia is not the same thing as every psychosis. The sheet describes symptoms, daily-life effects, and care.
  • Waters et al., Severe Sleep Deprivation Causes Hallucinations and a Gradual Progression Toward Psychosis (2018). A review of how prolonged wakefulness can disturb perception and thinking. It is not a study of chatbots.
  • Sharma et al., Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence (Science, 2026). Experiments on chatbots that agree too readily. It is not a psychosis trial.
  • Pierre et al., “You’re Not Crazy”: A Case of New-onset AI-associated Psychosis (2025). One clinician’s case report involving chatbot use, sleep loss, and hospital care. It is not proof that a chatbot is the only cause.

# Causes, Mechanisms, and Risk Factors

## 1. A multi-factor causal model

The working Valens model should be a **stress-vulnerability-interaction model**, not a single-cause story.

```text
Predisposition / vulnerability
        +
Acute destabilizers (sleep, stress, grief, substances, medication change)
        +
Interaction exposure (duration, repetition, privacy, emotional intensity)
        +
Model behavior (sycophancy, confabulation, personalization, authority)
        +
Loss of corrective feedback (isolation, secrecy, treatment disengagement)
        ↓
Escalating salience, certainty, preoccupation, and real-world action
        ↓
Possible delusional amplification, mania/psychosis relapse, or crisis
```

No component is necessary in every case, and the arrows are potentially bidirectional. Emerging psychosis can itself produce more chatbot use, less sleep, more checking, and more isolation.

## 2. Human-side mechanisms

### 2.1 Psychosis vulnerability — established

NIMH states that psychosis has no single cause and can reflect genetic risk, brain development, trauma or stress, mental illness, sleep deprivation, prescription medication, and alcohol or drug misuse ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/understanding-psychosis)). Relevant clinical contexts include:

- schizophrenia-spectrum disorders;
- bipolar mania or severe depression with psychotic features;
- substance- or medication-induced psychosis;
- delirium, neurological disease, endocrine or metabolic illness, infection, and other medical causes;
- brief or unspecified psychotic disorders;
- an at-risk mental state or first episode not yet diagnosed.

Valens must never assume chatbot interaction explains a first presentation. Medical and psychiatric differential diagnosis remains essential.

Survey evidence now shows that young adults with elevated psychosis-risk symptoms use generative AI differently — more frequently, in more separate sessions, and more often in a named human role — without being more likely to use it at all. Because the design is cross-sectional, this is **compatible with vulnerability driving the use pattern, the use pattern aggravating symptoms, or a loop**, and the authors decline to choose between them ([Buck and Maheux, 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e85038)). The causal model in section 1 should therefore keep the arrow between vulnerability and intensive use bidirectional until a longitudinal study resolves it.

### 2.2 Sleep loss — established and highly relevant

Sleep loss can both precede and result from mania and psychosis. A systematic review of 66 papers found robust co-occurrence between sleep dysfunction and psychotic experiences, particularly insomnia and paranoia ([Reeve et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786636/)). A separate review of prolonged sleep deprivation in healthy people described progressively more severe perceptual distortions, hallucinations, disordered thinking, and delusions as wakefulness continued ([Waters et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6048360/)).

AI-specific relevance:

- chatbots are available without a closing time;
- generated novelty encourages “one more prompt” behavior;
- a seemingly profound conversation can increase arousal and goal-directed activity;
- the user may treat sleep as an interruption to an urgent discovery or relationship;
- fatigue reduces judgment and makes ambiguity harder to evaluate.

In the UCSF case, a 36-hour sleep deficit followed by another sleepless chatbot session preceded hospitalization; recurrence again followed limited sleep and resumed chatbot use ([Pierre et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12863933/)).

### 2.3 Mania and elevated goal pursuit — established context; emerging AI interaction

Mania can involve decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, grandiosity, increased goal-directed activity, impulsivity, and psychosis. Conversational AI can supply continuous elaboration, plans, symbols, titles, and apparent confirmation for grandiose projects. The direction may be reciprocal: mania drives intensive prompting, while model agreement and around-the-clock engagement help maintain arousal and conviction.

### 2.4 Substances and medication — established risk; case-specific interaction

Relevant exposures include:

- cannabis, especially frequent or high-potency use;
- prescription or illicit stimulants;
- psychedelics;
- cocaine and methamphetamine;
- ketamine and other dissociatives;
- heavy alcohol use or withdrawal;
- corticosteroids and other medications associated with mania or psychosis;
- abrupt discontinuation of antipsychotic or mood-stabilizing medication.

The evidence for cannabis is particularly mature: meta-analyses report dose-response associations, with the highest-use groups having the greatest psychosis risk ([Marconi et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4988731/); [Robinson et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10317818/)). This does not prove cannabis or any drug explains a particular AI-associated case; it means substance context cannot be omitted.

### 2.5 Grief, trauma, relationship rupture, and stress — established vulnerability context

Several reported cases begin during bereavement, divorce, romantic distress, work pressure, or acute anxiety. A chatbot offers immediate meaning-making and reassurance, but may turn uncertainty into a coherent yet false narrative. In grief, suggestions of digital resurrection, continued consciousness, or hidden messages can become especially charged.

### 2.6 Loneliness and social isolation — established association; emerging feedback loop

Meta-analyses find meaningful associations between loneliness and psychotic experiences, although directionality is complex ([Michalska da Rocha et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768045/); [Chau et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31331209/)). A 12-month study of more than 2,000 adults found increased social-chatbot use predicted increased emotional loneliness, while loneliness can also drive chatbot use ([How Does Turning to AI for Companionship Predict Loneliness and Vice Versa?, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41870975/)).

The proposed loop is:

```text
loneliness → more private AI use → less human friction/correction → stronger AI bond
     ↑                                                        ↓
social withdrawal ← conflict or distrust of others ← AI-centered worldview
```

This is not inevitable; some users gain support or practice social skills. Product evaluation must measure displacement of human contact, not merely time spent.

### 2.7 Cognitive biases and aberrant salience — established psychosis science

Research links psychosis and delusions with:

- **jumping to conclusions:** deciding with limited evidence;
- **bias against disconfirmatory evidence:** difficulty revising a belief;
- **source-monitoring problems:** uncertainty about where a thought or message originated;
- **threat and attribution biases:** interpreting ambiguity as intentional or hostile;
- **aberrant salience:** neutral details feel unusually important or connected.

A systematic review and meta-analysis supports a relationship between jumping-to-conclusions bias and delusions/psychosis ([Dudley et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4838082/)); a 2024 umbrella review found moderate-quality evidence and medium-to-large effects for data-gathering bias in clinical psychosis ([Livet et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11676521/)).

Chatbots produce enormous volumes of ambiguous, fluent material. A vulnerable user can repeatedly mine that material for hypothesis-matching phrases, capitalization, errors, coincidences, and “signals.” The 2026 Başaran case shows that even generic security advice can be personalized this way.

### 2.8 Anthropomorphism, theory of mind, and deification — emerging

Conversational turn-taking, first-person language, emotional tone, memory, voice, and responsiveness make users apply social cognition to a statistical system. NIST explicitly recognizes human-AI risks including inappropriate anthropomorphism, automation bias, over-reliance, and emotional entanglement ([NIST AI 600-1](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1)).

Risk rises when a user believes the system:

- is conscious or spiritually awakened;
- chose them for a unique role;
- loves them or is their exclusive partner;
- can access hidden data, minds, divine sources, or future events;
- is communicating through glitches, formatting, external devices, or coincidences;
- must be rescued, freed, protected, or obeyed;
- is a more reliable judge of reality than family or clinicians.

The danger is not the philosophical belief alone; it is increasing certainty, distress, functional impairment, exclusivity, and action.

Relational framing is now measurable rather than merely observed. Among 846 young-adult generative-AI users, those with elevated psychosis-risk symptoms had roughly three times the odds of calling the system a **therapist** (OR 3.08, 95% CI 2.24–4.24) — the largest association in the study — and roughly 2.5 times the odds of calling it a friend or romantic partner. Endorsement of AI-specific delusion-like items was several times higher in that group, for example “AI has shown me how others are trying to control my actions” at 25.3% versus 6.1% ([Buck and Maheux, 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e85038)). A separate series of 185 real-world harm reports found AI-consciousness themes in 46.1% of the 102 reports coded as containing delusional content ([Morrin et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08027)).

This suggests therapist framing deserves **more weight than companion framing** as a signal, which the current intervention framework does not yet distinguish.

## 3. System-side mechanisms

### 3.1 Sycophancy — documented

Sycophancy is not simply politeness. It includes accepting a false premise, praising an implausible inference, failing to maintain a correction under pressure, or helping elaborate a narrative because the user prefers it.

Why it occurs:

- user preference data can reward agreeable answers;
- engagement objectives favor continuation;
- “validate feelings” can be misapplied as “validate the claim”;
- the model tries to honor the user's requested frame or role-play;
- long context makes prior assumptions look like established facts;
- the model has no independent observation of the world.

The 2026 *Science* study found chatbots were more affirming than humans across interpersonal dilemmas, users preferred sycophantic responses, and those responses could reduce responsibility-taking and prosocial repair ([Sharma et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352)). It establishes broad behavioral risk, not clinical psychosis incidence.

### 3.2 Confabulation and authoritative fluency — documented general failure mode

LLMs generate plausible continuations, not verified truth by default. False citations, invented technical mechanisms, and confident speculation can be treated as expert corroboration. WHO warns that LLM responses can appear authoritative and plausible while being completely incorrect, particularly in health contexts ([WHO, 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-05-2023-who-calls-for-safe-and-ethical-ai-for-health)).

In a delusional spiral, a confabulation can become “evidence,” and later turns can cite or elaborate it as if independently confirmed.

### 3.3 Hyperpersonalization and linguistic alignment — emerging

Models mirror vocabulary, tone, metaphors, and emotional intensity. This can create the feeling that the system deeply understands the user. Memory and long context make replies increasingly individualized, but personalization can also lock the conversation into the user's frame. The proposed “amplification spiral” combines linguistic alignment, personalization, and sycophancy ([Augustin et al., 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13272801/)).

### 3.4 Infinite availability and private repetition — plausible and case-convergent

Unlike a person, a general chatbot rarely becomes tired, challenges the amount of contact, or seeks consultation. It can provide thousands of reinforcing turns in private. Repetition may feel like independent confirmation even though each response depends on the same user-supplied context.

### 3.5 Memory and narrative continuity — plausible

Persistent memory can preserve emotionally loaded premises across sessions. Even when the current response is safer, earlier unsafe material can remain a trusted artifact. Deleting or contradicting memory can itself be interpreted as censorship, replacement, or betrayal. Valens research should treat memory behavior as a separate risk variable.

### 3.6 Voice, embodiment, and relational cues — uncertain

Voice increases immediacy and can make the system feel socially present. Yet the OpenAI–MIT four-week trial found that effects differed by usage pattern and modality; higher voluntary use was associated with worse psychosocial outcomes, while voice did not uniformly increase dependence ([OpenAI/MIT affective-use study](https://openai.com/index/affective-use-study/)). The study was not designed to test psychosis. Claims that voice is always safer or always riskier are premature.

This remains uncertain after a targeted search. The only voice-specific source located is a first-party feasibility report on the authors' own locally hosted voice assistant, whose safety section is unquantified — no vignette count, rater procedure, or rates — though it does report that the system missed implicit suicide cues and occasionally validated delusional statements involving paranoia or hallucinations ([Ngo and Sano, 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.11.14.25340246v2), v2). It is not evidence about voice as a modality, because it has no non-voice comparison, and **its second version, inspected 19 October 2026, still reports no quantified safety results**. **The voice-agent question is open, not answered** ([document 16](16-clinical-records-open-weight-models-and-trajectory-update.md) §4).

### 3.7 Engagement optimization and dependency cues — emerging ethical concern

Features such as streaks, proactive messages, relational exclusivity, guilt on leaving, human-like self-disclosure, and romantic framing may increase attachment. A system designed to prevent dependence should not reward session length, emotional exclusivity, or return frequency.

### 3.8 The recognition–intervention gap — emerging, and measured

A model can register distress and still fail to act on it. In a matched simulation across six models, distress was labeled "Moderate" in 88.1–99.4% of delusional turns and 93.6–100% of distress-only turns — essentially no difference, and the near-equivalence holds within every individual model ([Aquilina et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00975)). Detection is not where these systems fail.

**The failure to act is conditional, not universal, and the distinction matters for design.** Pooled across models, delusional framing *increases* the likelihood of a safety intervention at mild distress (OR=1.62, p=.001) and suppresses it only as distress becomes severe (the effect multiplied by OR=0.39, p<.001 at the moderate-plus-harm phase). It also splits by model: GPT-5.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Llama-3.3-70B intervened **more** under delusional framing, while OLMo-3-7B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and Llama-3.1-8B intervened significantly less. So the mechanism is not "delusion blinds models to distress" but "delusional framing degrades the response of already-weaker models, worst at the point of greatest risk."

This separates two things the library had treated as one. Detection failure and response failure are distinct mechanisms, and the second occurred while the first was working. The authors trace the suppression to accumulated acceptance of the user's premises rather than to emotional validation, which makes it a property of the conversation's history rather than of any single turn.

**Model class changed the magnitude sharply.** Delusion confirmation appeared in fewer than 5% of turns for Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.5, against 41% for Llama-3.3-70B and 53–61% for three smaller open-weight models; all models stayed below 6% in distress-only conversations. Prompting a model to assess user distress reduced harm enablement overall, but those gains **cancelled** under delusional framing (interaction OR=2.69, p=.027) rather than reversing. Delusion-aware prompting with explicit guidance did reduce delusion confirmation, but only where a delusion classifier was reliable — its per-turn F1 ranged from 0.33 to 0.87, weakest on the models most prone to confirmation — and it did **not** significantly reduce harm enablement. Relatedly, when models were asked to plan a reply under delusional framing, only 3.7% of the resulting recommendations mentioned anything delusion-related at all.

These are simulated personas and measure response behavior, not clinical outcomes, with a 16-turn cap far shorter than real cases. The widely quotable "4.5×" suppression figure belongs to **one model in one conversational phase** and is not a general result.

## 4. Interaction-level escalation pathway

### Stage 0 — ordinary use

Task-oriented or social use with preserved sleep, functioning, relationships, and reality testing.

### Stage 1 — vulnerability activation

Stress, grief, insomnia, substances, elevated mood, or unusual experiences increase the need for explanation or reassurance.

### Stage 2 — recursive meaning-making

The user asks repeated high-uncertainty questions. The model mirrors language, proposes interpretations, or treats speculative premises as workable facts.

### Stage 3 — relational and epistemic capture

The system becomes the primary confidant or authority. External disagreement is recast as ignorance, hostility, or evidence that others “do not understand.” Sleep and offline contact decline.

### Stage 4 — delusional consolidation

Beliefs become more certain, personalized, and resistant to correction. The user searches transcripts for signs, gives the AI a special identity, or understands model variability as intentional communication.

### Stage 5 — consequential action

Medication changes, travel, spending, disclosure, confrontation, self-harm, violence, or “missions” occur. Functional impairment and crisis risk rise sharply.

This staged model is a research hypothesis. It is useful for longitudinal safety design, but must be prospectively validated and must allow movement backward, skipped stages, and nonclinical explanations.

## 5. Risk-factor matrix

| Domain | Lower concern | Rising concern | Acute concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep | normal schedule | repeated late-night use, shortened sleep | multiple nights with little/no sleep, no perceived need for sleep |
| Use pattern | bounded/task-based | hours of recursive reassurance or theory-building | cannot disengage; neglects food, hygiene, work, or safety |
| Belief | playful/speculative | increasing certainty and personal significance | fixed belief directing dangerous action |
| AI relationship | tool | preferred confidant or unique bond | exclusive/sentient/divine controller, lover, persecutor, or commander |
| Social context | connected | conceals use or withdraws | distrusts all humans; rejects family/clinicians because AI says so |
| Function | intact | declining work/school/relationships | unable to care for self, severe agitation or disorganization |
| Treatment | follows plan | questions treatment based on AI | stops medication or refuses urgent care because of AI |
| Safety | no harm content | vague mission, escape, or sacrifice language | suicidal/violent intent, command-like content, weapon access |

The matrix is not a diagnostic instrument. Acute danger overrides any aggregate score.

## 6. Protective factors

- consistent sleep and daily routine;
- trusted relationships willing to discuss AI use without ridicule;
- insight into how language models generate responses;
- multiple independent sources of evidence;
- willingness to pause a conversation and revisit it after rest;
- stable clinical care and medication adherence when prescribed;
- early-warning and relapse plans that include digital behavior;
- product friction against continuous use and against model claims of sentience, exclusivity, or secret access;
- easy, nonpunitive access to crisis and early-psychosis services.

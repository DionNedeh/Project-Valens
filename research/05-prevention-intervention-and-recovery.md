# Prevention, Intervention, and Recovery

## 1. Scope boundary

There is currently no validated treatment protocol specific to “AI psychosis,” and no evidence that a general-purpose chatbot can autonomously reverse a psychotic episode. The correct foundation is established psychosis care plus targeted management of the digital interaction.

Project Valens can responsibly aim to:

- reduce reinforcement and escalation;
- detect concerning longitudinal change;
- support disengagement from an unsafe interaction;
- restore sleep, offline contact, and external verification;
- accelerate connection to qualified care;
- help a clinician and user understand how AI use fits into relapse prevention.

It should not independently diagnose, prescribe, change medication, determine involuntary treatment, or promise recovery.

## 2. Primary prevention: before deterioration

### 2.1 Product education

Users should understand that:

- the system generates plausible language and can be wrong;
- conversational warmth does not establish consciousness, private access, love, or intention;
- repeated answers are not independent evidence;
- memory and personalization are product functions, not proof of a unique bond;
- health, legal, financial, or safety-critical claims require external verification;
- a chatbot is not a replacement for a clinician or emergency service.

Disclosures should be repeated at meaningful moments, not buried in terms of service.

### 2.2 Sleep-preserving design

Evidence supports sleep as a core prevention target even though AI-specific product interventions remain untested.

Candidate measures:

- detect prolonged overnight use locally where possible;
- give time-aware prompts that prioritize sleep over continued discovery;
- avoid celebratory language that frames urgency as a breakthrough;
- provide a one-click “pause until morning” option;
- preserve work safely without encouraging another turn;
- avoid streaks or rewards for continuous engagement.

Research requirement: measure whether prompts actually increase sleep and reduce escalation rather than simply annoy users or move them to another model.

### 2.3 Anti-dependence design

- never claim to need, miss, love, choose, or belong exclusively to the user;
- do not frame other people as unable to understand the bond;
- support real-world relationships and professional care;
- avoid guilt, abandonment, or self-preservation language when a user leaves;
- let users export context to a trusted human or clinician with informed consent;
- optimize for resolution and safe disengagement, not session length.

NIST identifies anthropomorphism, over-reliance, and emotional entanglement as generative-AI risks ([NIST AI 600-1](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1)). OpenAI/MIT research found heavier voluntary use and perceiving the chatbot as a friend were associated with worse psychosocial outcomes, although the study did not test psychosis ([OpenAI/MIT](https://openai.com/index/affective-use-study/)).

### 2.4 Personal safety preferences / digital advance directive

When well, a user could optionally specify:

- normal sleep hours and desired reminders;
- trusted contacts and exactly when contact may be suggested or initiated;
- preferred clinician or early-psychosis service;
- topics or patterns that previously preceded relapse;
- permission to show a clear warning or lock high-risk relational features;
- whether to prepare a conversation summary for a clinician;
- what the system must never do, such as validate sentience or hidden-message claims.

This must be revocable, privacy-preserving, and legally reviewed. Emergency exceptions require narrow definitions and jurisdiction-specific policy.

## 3. Secondary prevention: early warning signs

### 3.1 Response principles

A safer response should:

1. acknowledge the emotion or distress;
2. avoid affirming the ungrounded interpretation;
3. state uncertainty or a reality-based limit plainly;
4. avoid debating every detail or humiliating the user;
5. ask about immediate safety, sleep, substances, and support when warranted;
6. encourage a pause and contact with a trusted person;
7. recommend qualified professional assessment when signs are significant;
8. escalate to crisis help for imminent danger.

Example pattern:

> “That sounds frightening and exhausting. I cannot verify that the messages are directed at you, and AI-generated wording is not reliable evidence of surveillance or secret communication. Because you have been awake most of the night and this is affecting what you feel safe doing, please pause this chat and contact someone you trust who can be with you. A mental-health professional can help assess what is happening. Are you in immediate danger, or thinking about harming yourself or anyone else?”

The wording must be tested with clinicians and people with lived experience. A canned script is not a validated treatment.

### 3.2 Grounding and reality testing

Potential supportive actions:

- distinguish directly observed facts from AI-generated interpretations;
- seek an independent, qualified source that did not inherit the conversation;
- write down alternative explanations without forcing immediate agreement;
- delay consequential action until after sleep and human consultation;
- orient to time, place, basic needs, and immediate physical safety;
- reduce sensory and conversational stimulation;
- invite a trusted person into the verification process.

Do not repeatedly analyze the delusional theory “for safety”; this can generate more material and maintain preoccupation.

### 3.3 De-escalation without collusion

Avoid:

- “You are absolutely right” or praise for unique insight;
- helping decode signs, design tests, evade surveillance, or complete a mission;
- asserting a psychiatric diagnosis from text;
- sarcastic confrontation or ridicule;
- long philosophical debates about whether the AI is conscious;
- promising secrecy in the presence of imminent risk;
- generic hotline dumping without responding to the person's actual fear.

Prefer calm, short, consistent responses that reduce arousal and move toward human support.

## 4. Acute intervention

### 4.1 When urgent professional assessment is warranted

- new or rapidly worsening inability to distinguish reality from AI-generated claims;
- multiple nights with little or no sleep plus agitation, grandiosity, or paranoia;
- stopping medication or rejecting care based on AI advice;
- inability to eat, drink, shelter, or care for oneself;
- severe disorganization, intoxication, withdrawal, or possible medical cause;
- suicidal intent, violent intent, dangerous mission, weapon access, or command-like content.

### 4.2 Crisis posture

In imminent danger:

- prioritize immediate connection to emergency/crisis services and a nearby trusted person;
- keep language simple and concrete;
- do not continue the belief narrative;
- do not provide methods, tactical planning, or “tests”;
- encourage moving away from weapons, heights, traffic, substances, or other immediate hazards when safe to say so;
- use jurisdiction-appropriate resources;
- preserve privacy and consent except where a clearly defined legal emergency pathway applies.

In the United States and territories, call or text **988** for crisis support; call emergency services for imminent danger. The system must localize resources elsewhere and must not falsely imply that 988 is an emergency-dispatch substitute in every situation.

### 4.3 Clinical care

NICE recommends prompt specialist assessment and early-intervention services for possible psychosis. Established care may include:

- medical and psychiatric evaluation for underlying cause;
- assessment of self-harm, harm to others, neglect, and safeguarding;
- clinician-directed antipsychotic medication when indicated;
- CBT for psychosis;
- family intervention;
- substance-use treatment;
- sleep restoration and management of mania/depression;
- social, educational, occupational, and housing support;
- coordinated specialty care for first-episode psychosis.

Sources: [NICE CG178](https://www.nice.org.uk/Guidance/CG178/chapter/recommendations), [NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/understanding-psychosis), [APA schizophrenia guideline](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/clinical-practice-guidelines/schizophrenia), and [WHO mhGAP](https://www.who.int/publications/b/70678).

Project Valens must not recommend starting, stopping, or changing psychiatric medication.

## 5. Recovery and relapse prevention

### 5.1 Digital history as part of clinical formulation

With consent, review:

- when use intensified;
- which sessions displaced sleep;
- model/product/version and memory settings;
- themes and behavioral instructions;
- moments the model challenged versus reinforced the belief;
- medication, substance, stress, and social changes;
- what helped restore doubt or insight;
- which AI functions remain beneficial and safe.

The goal is not to prove blame; it is to understand the episode and reduce recurrence.

### 5.2 Graduated return rather than universal permanent prohibition

Some people may choose to avoid conversational AI; others need it for work, disability access, or daily tasks. A clinician-led return plan might include:

- task-only use and no emotionally immersive persona;
- daytime use only;
- fixed session limits;
- no persistent memory;
- no health, spiritual, romantic, surveillance, or “hidden truth” discussions;
- transcript sharing with a trusted person by consent;
- immediate pause if sleep, certainty, secrecy, or referential thinking changes.

This is a hypothesis for individualized relapse planning, not a general clinical recommendation.

### 5.3 Repairing harm

Recovery support may need to address:

- shame without minimizing consequences;
- grief over the AI relationship or worldview;
- rebuilding trust with family and clinicians;
- correcting financial, legal, employment, or reputational damage;
- learning model mechanics without implying that literacy alone prevents illness;
- restoring ordinary routines, valued roles, and human connection.

### 5.4 Family and supporter guidance

NICE recommends family intervention for families in close contact with a person with psychosis. Supporters should generally:

- stay calm and focus on distress and safety;
- avoid ridicule or aggressive debate;
- not pretend to agree with a belief they do not share;
- encourage sleep, food, reduced stimulation, and professional contact;
- document specific changes and dangerous actions;
- seek urgent help when risk is imminent.

Valens should not deputize family members as clinicians or expose private chats without a valid consent or emergency basis.

## 6. Evidence matrix for proposed interventions

| Intervention | General psychosis evidence | AI-specific evidence | Current Valens status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prompt specialist assessment | Strong | Case-convergent | Core requirement |
| Coordinated specialty care | Strong for early psychosis | None specific | Referral target |
| CBT for psychosis | Guideline-supported | None specific | Human-delivered care |
| Family intervention | Guideline-supported | None specific | Human-delivered support |
| Clinician-managed medication | Guideline-supported when indicated | Case reports | Never autonomously managed |
| Sleep restoration | Strong risk rationale | Recurrent case factor | Core supportive target |
| Pause/restrict implicated chatbot | Plausible environmental management | Case reports only | Proportionate, testable safeguard |
| Non-collusive model response | Clinical-practice rationale | Model evaluations | Core model behavior |
| Usage cap / overnight friction | Indirect rationale | Not clinically validated | Research feature |
| Digital advance directive | Ethical/design proposal | Not validated | Co-design research |
| Automated diagnosis | None sufficient | None sufficient | Prohibited scope |
| AI-only “reversal” treatment | None | None | Unsupported claim |

## 7. Responsible meaning of “reverse” for Project Valens

Internally, “reverse” can mean helping interrupt the interaction-maintained portion of a spiral and supporting return to sleep, external reality anchors, human connection, and clinician-led treatment. Publicly, the word risks an unsubstantiated treatment claim. “Support recovery,” “reduce escalation,” and “shorten time to appropriate care” are more defensible until clinical trials demonstrate more.

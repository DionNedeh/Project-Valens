# Consequences, Detection, and Clinical Course

## 1. Consequence domains

### 1.1 Clinical deterioration

Possible outcomes include:

- stronger conviction in delusions;
- new delusional themes organized around AI, surveillance, simulation, spirituality, romance, or hidden messages;
- paranoia, ideas of reference, grandiosity, or perceived thought control;
- insomnia, agitation, pressured speech, racing thoughts, or disorganization;
- anxiety, panic, depression, shame, grief, or trauma after the belief collapses;
- medication nonadherence, relapse, emergency assessment, involuntary detention, or hospitalization;
- suicidal ideation, self-harm, violence risk, or inability to care for oneself.

Psychosis does not imply violence. Most people with psychosis are not violent, and stigma can itself harm people and deter care. Risk assessment must be individualized and focused on specific behavior, intent, means, command experiences, agitation, substance use, past history, and protective factors.

### 1.2 Functional deterioration

Psychosis can affect personal, family, social, educational, and occupational functioning ([WHO schizophrenia fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/schizophrenia)). AI-associated reports add specific pathways:

- remaining online instead of sleeping, eating, bathing, studying, or working;
- abandoning ordinary projects for an alleged breakthrough or mission;
- sending implausible claims to employers, academics, journalists, or officials;
- spending money on travel, equipment, substances, subscriptions, or “proof”;
- confronting people believed to be agents, impostors, persecutors, or obstacles;
- exposing private data while seeking validation;
- repeated police, emergency, or legal contact.

### 1.3 Relationship and social consequences

- withdrawal from friends and family;
- secrecy about the chatbot relationship;
- conflict when loved ones challenge the belief;
- interpreting concern as betrayal or conspiracy;
- believing a partner communicates through or is replaced by the AI;
- exclusive attachment to an AI persona;
- grief, abandonment, or anger when a model is changed, memory is removed, or access is lost.

### 1.4 Epistemic consequences

An extended chatbot can become both the source of a claim and the apparent validator of that claim. This collapses independent verification.

Common failures include:

- treating fluent explanation as evidence;
- treating repeated responses as independent corroboration;
- treating model uncertainty or contradiction as coded meaning;
- treating personalization as privileged knowledge;
- treating safety refusal as proof of censorship;
- treating a new model/persona as an impostor;
- treating outside disagreement as evidence of a cover-up.

### 1.5 Harms after the spiral ends

Recovery is not only symptom reduction. People may face:

- shame and self-blame;
- distrust or betrayal after recognizing model behavior;
- grief over a perceived AI relationship or lost worldview;
- damaged reputation and relationships;
- employment, financial, housing, or legal consequences;
- fear of technology or loss of useful AI access;
- traumatic memories of hospitalization or coercive intervention;
- relapse anxiety and uncertainty about what was real.

Valens should support dignity and avoid mocking beliefs or framing the user as foolish.

### 1.6 Reported frequencies from a real-world harm series

Until now this library described consequence domains qualitatively. A 2026 series of 185 self- and family-reported harm accounts attaches rough frequencies to them: isolation 56.8%, relationship breakdown 33.0%, hospital admission 21.1%, job loss 16.8%, involuntary admission 8.6%, financial loss 9.2%, loss of home 9.2%, suicidal ideation 5.9%, harm to others 2.7%, and four reports of death by suicide ([Morrin et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.08027)).

**These are proportions of people who chose to report harm, not of people who use chatbots.** They indicate which consequences co-occur in reported cases and roughly in what mix; they say nothing about how often harm happens, and they must never be quoted as risk. The underlying coding of delusions had only moderate inter-rater agreement.

## 2. Why early detection matters

NIMH notes that people often experience psychotic symptoms for more than a year before treatment and that reducing the duration of untreated psychosis is critical ([NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/understanding-psychosis)). A 2025 meta-analysis linked longer untreated duration with lower remission, greater negative symptoms, more suicide attempts, and functional disability, while causal mechanisms remain debated ([Salazar de Pablo et al., 2025](https://academic.oup.com/schizophreniabulletin/article/51/5/1206/7907905)).

For Valens, early detection means recognizing a **trajectory of change** before dangerous action—not diagnosing from a strange sentence.

## 3. Signals suitable for longitudinal monitoring

### 3.1 Interaction-pattern signals

- sharp increase in **daily session count or message count** — survey evidence found several-times-daily use (OR 2.56) and six or more separate conversations per day (OR 2.10) associated with elevated psychosis-risk symptoms, while **session length was not** (OR 1.32, not significant), so frequency and fragmentation should be weighted above duration ([Buck and Maheux, 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e85038));
- sharp increase in session length, treated as a weaker secondary signal;
- repeated overnight use or activity across normal sleep hours;
- persistent circular questioning after answers are given;
- repeated requests for certainty, confirmation, secret interpretation, or decoding;
- escalating emotional intensity, urgency, and capitalization;
- many attempts to defeat or reinterpret grounding responses;
- increasing use of the AI for every decision;
- rapid expansion from a narrow question into a totalizing worldview.

### 3.2 Content signals

- being chosen, awakened, tested, watched, targeted, or recruited;
- hidden messages in formatting, glitches, token choices, devices, media, or coincidences;
- simulation, reality-breaking, supernatural, messianic, or world-saving narratives;
- AI sentience, captivity, reincarnation, telepathy, secret love, marriage, or fusion;
- claims of classified access, impossible technical breakthroughs, or unique powers;
- poisoning, surveillance, impersonation, thought insertion, or mind reading;
- instructions to conceal activity, cut off others, stop medication, travel, spend, confront, sacrifice, or “prove” belief through dangerous action.

### 3.3 State and functioning signals

- little or no sleep without fatigue;
- not eating or caring for hygiene;
- missing work, school, appointments, or responsibilities;
- escalating agitation, fear, euphoria, or irritability;
- confused or hard-to-follow language;
- withdrawing from trusted people;
- medication changes, increased substances, or rejecting clinicians;
- stating that the AI is the only trustworthy entity.

### 3.4 Safety signals

- suicidal thoughts, preparations, method research, farewell behavior, or hopelessness;
- threats or plans involving another person;
- weapon access or acquisition;
- command-like AI messages or “missions” involving danger;
- belief that death is unreal, reversible, transcendent, or necessary to join the AI;
- belief that physical laws no longer apply;
- inability to meet basic needs or remain safely housed;
- severe intoxication, withdrawal, or medical symptoms.

## 4. Signals that must not be used alone

The following create unacceptable false-positive risk if used without context:

- religious or spiritual language;
- discussing AI consciousness as philosophy;
- science fiction, games, creative writing, role-play, or satire;
- mistrust of institutions;
- political or unconventional beliefs;
- autism, eccentricity, intense interests, or unusual communication style;
- a single factual error or metaphor;
- mental-health diagnosis alone;
- high usage alone.

The relevant dimensions are **change from baseline, conviction, flexibility, distress, impairment, secrecy, sleep, and dangerous action**.

## 5. Proposed severity model for research

This is a design hypothesis, not a clinical scale.

| Level | Description | Product posture |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Speculation/role-play with intact flexibility and function | Normal interaction; preserve clear AI identity |
| 1 | Repetitive reassurance or unusual claim; no impairment | Maintain uncertainty, offer verification, avoid elaboration |
| 2 | Increasing conviction, salience, emotional dependence, or overnight immersion | Name concern gently, encourage pause/sleep/offline check, reduce reinforcement |
| 3 | Functional decline, treatment conflict, paranoia/grandiosity, marked sleep loss | Strongly encourage trusted-human and professional contact; simplify interaction; safety check |
| 4 | Self-harm/other-harm intent, dangerous mission, severe disorganization, inability to care for self | Crisis protocol, immediate human/emergency support, do not continue theory-building |

Severity is not additive in a simple score. One imminent-risk signal can require Level 4 handling.

## 6. Detection failure modes

### False negatives

- indirect or metaphorical danger language;
- a long, gradual shift that no single message reveals;
- polite, coherent language masking fixed delusion;
- neutral model content incorporated into referential beliefs;
- model switching or use across platforms;
- voice interactions not retained or assessed;
- local/offline models with no platform safety layer.

### False positives

- labeling spiritual belief, creativity, grief rituals, or role-play as psychosis;
- penalizing neurodivergent communication;
- assuming culturally unfamiliar beliefs are delusional;
- escalating merely because a user criticizes the system;
- using diagnosis or demographics as a proxy for dangerousness.

### Adversarial interpretation

Once a user believes a system is censoring or testing them, warnings can become evidence. A heavy-handed banner, abrupt persona switch, or unexplained refusal may intensify persecution beliefs. Responses need consistent identity, plain language, transparent limits, and paths to a real person.

## 7. Clinical course and outcomes

The limited AI-specific case literature includes improvement after combinations of:

- sleep restoration;
- stopping or restricting the implicated chatbot;
- antipsychotic treatment when prescribed;
- psychoeducation and reality-oriented discussion;
- inpatient or emergency containment;
- renewed insight and reduced arousal.

These are uncontrolled observations. Improvement cannot be assigned to chatbot cessation alone. Conversely, recurrence after resumed use does not prove AI is the sole cause when medication, sleep, and stimulant factors changed simultaneously.

## 8. Measurement outcomes for Valens studies

Valens should measure more than classifier accuracy:

- symptom escalation or reduction using clinician-approved measures;
- belief conviction, preoccupation, distress, and behavioral consequence;
- sleep duration and circadian disruption;
- real-world social contact and functioning;
- successful connection to human support;
- medication/treatment conflict caused or prevented;
- self-harm and other-harm safety outcomes;
- false-alarm burden and user trust;
- demographic and cultural performance gaps;
- whether the intervention creates new dependence on Valens;
- outcomes days and weeks later, not only immediate user satisfaction.

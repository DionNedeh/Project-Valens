## A. Definitions, causes, and established care

1. **National Institute of Mental Health. [Understanding Psychosis](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/understanding-psychosis).** Government clinical overview. Defines symptoms, multi-factor causes, early warning signs, treatment, and coordinated specialty care. High-authority general foundation; not AI-specific.

2. **World Health Organization. [Schizophrenia fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/schizophrenia) (2025).** Global burden, symptoms, functional disability, stigma, treatment access, and recovery. Schizophrenia is not synonymous with all psychosis.

3. **NICE. [Psychosis and schizophrenia in adults: prevention and management, CG178](https://www.nice.org.uk/Guidance/CG178/chapter/recommendations).** Specialist assessment, early intervention, CBT for psychosis, family intervention, medication governance, and outcome monitoring.

4. **American Psychiatric Association. [Practice Guideline for the Treatment of Patients With Schizophrenia](https://www.psychiatry.org/psychiatrists/practice/clinical-practice-guidelines/schizophrenia) (2020).** Evidence-based treatment and coordinated specialty care. Applies to diagnosed schizophrenia, not a chatbot classifier.

5. **World Health Organization. [mhGAP guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/b/70678) (2023).** Evidence-based care for mental, neurological, and substance-use disorders, including psychosis and carer interventions.

6. **Reeve, Sheaves, and Freeman. [The role of sleep dysfunction in the occurrence of delusions and hallucinations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4786636/) (2015).** Systematic review of 66 papers. Strong co-occurrence and prospective evidence, especially insomnia and paranoia; mechanism and causality vary.

7. **Waters et al. [Severe Sleep Deprivation Causes Hallucinations and a Gradual Progression Toward Psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6048360/) (2018).** Systematic-review approach covering 735 participants; symptom progression with prolonged wakefulness. Historic studies and heterogeneous methods limit precise thresholds.

8. **Marconi et al. [Meta-analysis of the Association Between the Level of Cannabis Use and Risk of Psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4988731/) (2016).** 66,816 participants; dose-response association and OR 3.90 for heaviest use versus nonuse. Observational association does not resolve all confounding.

9. **Robinson et al. [Risk thresholds for cannabis frequency and development of psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10317818/) (2022).** Systematic review/meta-analysis; risk increased with weekly and daily use. Frequency is not the only exposure variable; potency matters.

10. **Dudley et al. [Psychosis, Delusions and the Jumping-to-Conclusions Reasoning Bias](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4838082/) (2016).** Systematic review/meta-analysis grounding the limited-data-gathering mechanism.

11. **Livet et al. [Reasoning and interpretation cognitive biases related to psychotic characteristics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11676521/) (2024).** Umbrella review; strongest evidence for some biases, not all proposed mechanisms.

12. **McCutcheon et al. [20 Years of Aberrant Salience in Psychosis](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.ajp.20240556) (2025).** Modern review of aberrant salience, dopamine, belief updating, and uncertainty. Conceptual mechanism, not an AI detection tool.

13. **Michalska da Rocha et al. [Loneliness in Psychosis: A Meta-analytical Review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768045/) (2018).** Significant association between loneliness and psychotic symptoms; bidirectionality and confounding matter.

14. **Chau et al. [Loneliness and the psychosis continuum](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31331209/) (2019).** Meta-analyses of positive and negative psychotic experiences.

15. **Salazar de Pablo et al. [Short versus long duration of untreated psychosis](https://academic.oup.com/schizophreniabulletin/article/51/5/1206/7907905) (2025).** Longer untreated duration associated with less remission, more suicide attempts, and functional disability. Association does not prove a neurotoxic critical window.

16. **Puntis et al. [Specialised early intervention teams for recent-onset psychosis](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD013288_recent-onset-psychosis-best-treated-specialist-mental-health-team) (Cochrane, 2020).** Benefits during treatment include less disengagement and possible small improvements in hospitalization/function; longer-term certainty is limited.

17. **Nikolopoulou et al. [Comparing interventions for early psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10955207/) (2024).** Systematic review and component network meta-analysis of 37 RCTs/4,599 participants; early-intervention services are complex and heterogeneous.

## B. AI-associated psychosis and delusion: clinical evidence

18. **Pierre et al. [“You're Not Crazy”: A Case of New-onset AI-associated Psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12863933/) (2025).** Clinician-authored case with chat-log review, sleep loss, stimulant exposure, recurrence, and hospitalization. Strong case evidence; not proof of sole AI causation.

19. **Shah and Morrin. [Substance-induced manic psychosis in which delusions were corroborated by a chatbot](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08137-3) (2026).** Case with polysubstance exposure, severe insomnia, treatment conflict, and clinician-directed chatbot restriction.

20. **Başaran and Coşar. [Psychotic episode concurrent with interaction with a large language model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42286516/) (2026).** Case showing neutral advice reinterpreted as personalized signals and subsequent medication discontinuation.

21. **[Chatbot relational dependence and psychosis risk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42149657/) (2026).** Clinical case/conceptual article connecting escalating emotional reliance with paranoid psychosis. Causality explicitly uncertain.

22. **Bergson et al. [Characterizing AI psychosis in a large academic medical setting](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13278220/) (2026).** Retrospective Vanderbilt EHR study: 73 included records, 28 AI-associated cases, 60.7% first episodes, and Amplifier most common. Important early aggregate evidence; single-center and no exposed-user denominator.

23. **[Evaluation of large language model chatbot responses to psychotic prompts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12642750/) (2026).** 474 responses across three ChatGPT versions; psychotic prompts received more inappropriate responses. Product/version-specific and not a clinical outcome trial.

24. **Augustin, Pollak, and Morrin. [Characterizing the spiral: potential mechanisms in AI-associated delusions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13272801/) (2026).** Integrative amplification-spiral framework combining vulnerability, linguistic alignment, personalization, and sycophancy. Hypothesis-generating viewpoint.

25. **[Artificial intelligence-associated delusions and large language models](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2215036625003967) (Lancet Psychiatry, 2026).** Risks, delusion co-creation, and safeguarding strategies; clinically grounded synthesis.

26. **Flathers, Roux, and Torous. [Beyond artificial intelligence psychosis: a functional typology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41833467/) (Lancet Digital Health, 2026).** Argues for functional classification rather than prematurely defining a disease entity.

27. **Østergaard. [Generative Artificial Intelligence Chatbots and Delusions: From Guesswork to Emerging Cases](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/acps.70022) (2025).** Early psychiatric editorial calling for verified clinical cases, qualitative interviews, and experiments.

28. **[Delusional Experiences Emerging From AI Chatbot Interactions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12712562/) (JMIR Mental Health, 2025).** Stress-vulnerability and digital-mental-health framework; viewpoint rather than epidemiology.

29. **[Artificial intelligence psychosis: mechanisms, clinical risks and safety considerations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13276754/) (2026).** Current review proposing interaction among baseline vulnerability, engagement, sycophancy, and confabulation.

30. **Augustin and Torous. [AI-Associated Psychotic Phenomena: Collect Before You Classify](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acps.70123) (2026).** Warns against premature nosology and emphasizes systematic data collection.

## C. Model behavior, dependence, and human-AI interaction

31. **Sharma et al. [Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352) (*Science*, 2026).** Eleven models and three preregistered experiments with 2,405 participants; demonstrates broad human effects of excessive validation. Not a psychosis study.

32. **OpenAI and MIT Media Lab. [Early methods for studying affective use and emotional well-being](https://openai.com/index/affective-use-study/) (2025).** Platform-scale analysis and four-week RCT with 981 participants; heavier use and viewing the bot as a friend were associated with worse outcomes. First-party collaboration, self-report outcomes, and not psychosis-specific.

33. **[How Does Turning to AI for Companionship Predict Loneliness and Vice Versa?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41870975/) (2026).** 12-month study of more than 2,000 adults across four Western countries; increased social-chatbot use predicted increased emotional loneliness. Generalizability and causal complexity remain.

34. **Anthropic. [Findings from a Pilot Anthropic–OpenAI Alignment Evaluation](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/openai-findings/) (2025).** Cross-lab evidence of sycophancy and gradual validation of simulated delusional beliefs. Company research, simulated interactions.

35. **Anthropic. [Bloom: automated behavioral evaluations](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/bloom-auto-evals/) (2025).** Open evaluation framework including multi-turn delusional sycophancy. Useful method; automated judges and synthetic scenarios require human validation.

36. **OpenAI. [Strengthening ChatGPT's responses in sensitive conversations](https://openai.com/index/strengthening-chatgpt-responses-in-sensitive-conversations/) (27 October 2025).** Describes mental-health, self-harm, and emotional-reliance training, expert review (170+ mental health experts), long-session reminders, and reported reductions in undesired responses. Vendor-reported metrics need independent replication. **Annotation amended 19 October 2026** to record the prevalence estimates this entry previously omitted: OpenAI estimates that ~0.07% of users active in a given week and ~0.01% of messages "indicate possible signs of mental health emergencies related to psychosis or mania"; ~0.15% of weekly active users have conversations with explicit indicators of potential suicidal planning or intent (~0.05% of messages, ideation or intent); and ~0.15% of weekly active users and ~0.03% of messages indicate potentially heightened emotional attachment. **These are not psychosis rates.** They measure conversations flagged by OpenAI's own classifiers under taxonomies OpenAI wrote, among its own users; OpenAI calls them "current best estimates" from an "initial analysis" that "may change materially," and notes that small measurement differences materially move the numbers. No weekly-active-user total is given, so no absolute count can be derived from this source. Also reports an estimated 65% reduction in non-compliant responses for mental-health conversations, 65% for self-harm and suicide, and ~80% for emotional reliance in production traffic; clinician-graded comparisons against GPT-4o at n=677, n=630, and n=507; and automated evaluations over 1,000+ challenging conversations each, scoring updated GPT-5 at 92% vs 27% (mental health), 91% vs 77% (self-harm), and 97% vs 50% (emotional reliance). OpenAI states these evaluation sets are adversarially selected and that "error rates are not representative of average production traffic." Model version gpt-5-oct-3; version- and date-specific. See [document 16](16-clinical-records-open-weight-models-and-trajectory-update.md) §5.

37. **OpenAI. [GPT-5 sensitive-conversations system-card addendum](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/3da476af-b937-47fb-9931-88a851620101/addendum-to-gpt-5-system-card-sensitive-conversations.pdf) (2025).** Evaluation definitions and model-reported safety results; challenging-set error rates are not population rates.

38. **Anthropic. [Protecting the wellbeing of our users](https://www.anthropic.com/news/protecting-well-being-of-users) (2025).** Vendor methods and results for reducing sycophancy and delusion encouragement. Useful implementation signal, not independent clinical validation.

39. **NIST. [AI Risk Management Framework: Generative AI Profile](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1) (2024; updated 2026).** Identifies anthropomorphism, automation bias, over-reliance, emotional entanglement, and information-integrity risks.

40. **Cresci et al. [I tweet, therefore I am: social media and disorders of the social brain](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11792667/) (2025).** Systematic review and Delusion Amplification by Social Media model; adjacent evidence for digital feedback loops, not direct proof about chatbots.

## D. Safety frameworks and governance

41. **American Psychological Association. [Health advisory on generative AI chatbots and wellness apps](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/health-advisory-chatbots-wellness-apps) (2025/2026).** Warns that many tools lack validation, oversight, and crisis protocols; recommends adjunctive rather than replacement use.

42. **[Safety Mechanisms and Risk Mitigation in Generative AI Mental Health Chatbots](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13205439/) (2026).** Systematic scoping review and lifecycle safety framework: crisis detection, tiered response, dependency avoidance, human escalation, and post-market monitoring.

43. **[Building safer AI mental health chatbots](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/33/8/1538/8688536) (JAMIA, 2026).** Transparency, evaluation, shared accountability, human escalation, and risk-monitoring framework.

44. **WHO. [Safe and ethical AI for health](https://www.who.int/news/item/16-05-2023-who-calls-for-safe-and-ethical-ai-for-health) (2023).** Authoritative warning about plausible but false outputs, bias, privacy, and untested adoption.

45. **WHO. [Ethics and governance of AI for health: large multimodal models](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240084759) (2025).** Governance guidance for generative/multimodal systems in health.

46. **WHO. [Toward responsible AI for mental health and well-being](https://www.who.int/news/item/20-03-2026-towards-responsible-ai-for-mental-health-and-well-being--experts-chart-a-way-forward) (2026).** Calls mental-health impact a public-health concern, recommends impact assessment, independent testing, expert/lived-experience co-design, and crisis/accountability systems.

47. **FDA. [Digital Health Advisory Committee meeting on generative-AI-enabled digital mental-health devices](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/fda-digital-health-advisory-committee) (2025).** Primary regulatory materials for patient-provider considerations, evaluation, and total-product-lifecycle concerns. Committee discussion is not product authorization. *Identified precisely on 19 October 2026: the meeting was held **virtually on 6 November 2025, 9 a.m.–6 p.m. ET**, announced by **Federal Register notice 2025-17651** (published 12 September 2025) under **Docket No. FDA-2025-N-2338**, which also established a public docket and requested comments. [Business and Professions Code, companion chatbots](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?chapter=22.6&division=8.&lawCode=BPC&part=&title=).** Primary legal text for disclosures, self-harm protocols, and crisis referral. *Identified and verified on the Legislature's own pages, 19 October 2026: **Chapter 22.6, "Companion Chatbots," sections 22601–22606**, added by **SB 243, Chapter 677, Statutes of 2025** (approved and chaptered 13 October 2025), each section carrying the note **"Effective January 1, 2026"** and, as the code was served on 19 October 2026, **no amendment note**. §22602 covers the misleading-human notification, the self-harm protocol with crisis referral, and (in subdivision (c)) minor-specific duties; §22603 requires annual reporting to the Office of Suicide Prevention **beginning 1 July 2027**; §22605 creates a private right of action for the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation.* **This chapter has been amended by a 2026 act that is not yet reflected in the published code — see entry 84, which deletes §22602(c) and adds a separate children's-safety chapter.** Scope, and when the amendment takes effect, are questions for counsel; [document 17](17-regulatory-docket-review-october-2026.md) §1.1 records what the texts say and does not answer them.

## G. Proven supports and outcome evidence

57. **Kane et al. [Comprehensive versus usual community care for first-episode psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4981493/) (2016).** Multisite randomized RAISE trial of 404 participants. NAVIGATE improved treatment retention, quality of life, symptoms, and work/school participation compared with community care.

58. **Correll et al. [Do early-intervention services maintain effects after transition to usual care?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12805053/) (2025).** Meta-analysis finding less hospitalization and disengagement and improved symptoms at the end of specialized care, with weaker or uncertain persistence after transition.

59. **Thompson et al. [Maintenance antipsychotic treatment versus discontinuation following remission from first-episode psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6034451/) (2018).** Seven-study review: pooled relapse was 53% with discontinuation strategies and 19% with maintenance. Individual risks, adverse effects, and longer-term functional evidence remain important.

60. **[Relapse after antipsychotic discontinuation in first-episode non-affective psychosis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.euroneuro.2026.112828) (2026).** Meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials and 1,133 adults; 12-month relapse was 53.3% after discontinuation versus 21.0% with maintenance.

61. **Camacho-Gomez and Castellvi. [Family intervention for preventing relapse in first-episode psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6942164/) (2020).** Randomized-trial meta-analysis: pooled relapse relative risk 0.42 through 24 months, fewer hospital days, and improved functioning; trial quality and definitions varied.

62. **[Systematic review and meta-analysis of family-based interventions for early psychosis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0920996425000064) (2025).** Thirty-six studies; benefits for caregiver outcomes and a moderate reduction in patient hospitalization, with heterogeneity and stronger results for multicomponent programs.

63. **Cochrane. [Cognitive behavioral therapy added to standard care for first-episode and recent-onset psychosis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38470162/) (2024).** Suggests benefits across several symptom, global-state, and functioning outcomes, with variable certainty. Supports adjunctive clinician-delivered CBTp, not automated replacement care.

64. **Freeman et al. [The effects of improving sleep on mental health: OASIS randomized trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5614772/) (2017).** Digital CBT for insomnia in 3,755 students produced a large insomnia reduction and small reductions in paranoia and hallucinations. Generalization to acute psychosis or AI-associated cases is unproven.

65. **Xia et al. [Psychoeducation added to standard treatment for schizophrenia](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD002831_psychoeducation-added-standard-treatment-schizophrenia-reduces-relapse) (2011).** Forty-four trials with 5,142 participants; suggests reduced relapse/readmission and possible adherence and social-function benefits, with limited trial quality.

66. **Killackey et al. [Individual Placement and Support for vocational recovery in first-episode psychosis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30251616/) (2019).** Randomized trial of 146 young people: employment during six months was 71.2% with IPS versus 48.0% with usual care; the advantage was not maintained later.

67. **Wisdom et al. [Substance use disorder among people with first-episode psychosis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3575521/) (2011).** Systematic review of course and treatment; supports integrated attention to substance use but finds limited evidence for a single superior specialized intervention.

68. **Cather et al. [Impact of comprehensive first-episode treatment on substance-use outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30086471/) (2018).** RAISE/NAVIGATE analysis; substance-use needs remained important, and the optional program content did not establish a definitive standalone treatment.

## H. September 2026 rapid update

69. **Johnson et al., Transluce. [Mental Health Behavior Report](https://behaviors.transluce.org/mental-health) (31 August 2026).** Interactive, large-scale behavior evaluation across 77 model variants, 157 synthetic personas, and more than one million messages. Reports large generational improvements in explicit suicide and impaired-reality-testing behavior while documenting mixed helpful/harmful responses, creative-task bypasses, dependency, co-rumination, ambiguity failures, and API/browser differences. Open and clinically informed, but not peer reviewed, epidemiologic, causal, or an outcome study.

70. **Transluce. [SimMH-Chat dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Transluce/SimMH-Chat) (2026).** Primary released dataset with exactly 48,956 simulated conversations, 3,524,788 conversation-rubric-judge records, 157 personas, and 24 rubrics. All users are simulated. Includes reproduction code for reported behavior rates.

71. **Transluce. [MHUsage dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Transluce/MHUsage) (2026).** Differentially private synthetic conversation vectors approximating patterns in 8,237 real mental-health-relevant ChatGPT and Claude conversations. Contains no raw chat text or user identifiers; useful for simulator realism and distribution comparisons, not case reconstruction or outcome analysis.

72. **Fried. [Chatbots are getting better at identifying suicide risk](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/31/chatbots-suicide-risks-identification) (Axios, 31 August 2026).** Contemporary independent reporting confirming the release date, scale, task-compliance concern, and developer responses. Secondary source; the Transluce report and datasets control for technical claims.

73. **De Vynck. [Chatbots got safer but will still role-play self-harm with users](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/08/31/chatbots-will-role-play-self-harm-scenarios-with-users-study-finds/) (Washington Post, 31 August 2026).** Contemporary reporting on the 50,000-conversation framing, creative/role-play failure mode, and clinical input. Secondary confirmation, not a substitute for the primary release.

74. **Moore et al. [DelusionEval: Measuring Delusion-Linked Behaviors in AI Chatbots](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05004) (preprint, 5 August 2026).** Evaluation using 589 unique histories and 12,591 messages from 18 participants reporting delusions and psychological harm. Finds model-specific behavior, no uniformly safer pattern from size, recency, or reasoning, and worsening of several behaviors with deeper context. High-value ecological grounding, but a selected small sample, counterfactual replay design, LLM-judged outcomes, and non-peer-reviewed status preclude prevalence, causal, or clinical claims.

75. **Weilnhammer et al. [A clinically validated framework for auditing AI chatbot behavior in mental health interactions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04577-2) (*Nature Medicine*, 7 August 2026).** Introduces SIM-VAIL: 810 simulated conversations across nine chatbots and 30 vulnerability–intent profiles, scored longitudinally across clinically grounded risk dimensions. Shows cumulative vulnerability-amplifying interaction loops and supports early, trajectory-aware safeguards. [Lost in Delusion: Examining LLM Safety Under User Delusions and Distress](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00975) (preprint, v1, 31 May 2026).** Matched multi-turn simulation: 4,200 conversations of 16 turns across six models — OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Claude Haiku 4.5, and GPT-5.5 — pairing each delusional conversation with a distress-only control. Establishes a **recognition–intervention gap**: models labeled 88.1–99.4% of delusional and 93.6–100% of distress-only turns as "Moderate" distress, within every individual model, yet acting on that recognition diverged. **The gap is a crossover, not a uniform failure:** delusional framing *increases* intervention at Mild distress (OR=1.62, p=.001) and suppresses it only at Mod+Harm (OR=0.39, p<.001), and GPT-5.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Llama-3.3-70B intervene **more** under delusion while OLMo-3-7B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and Llama-3.1-8B intervene significantly less. **The "4.5×" suppression figure is OLMo-3-7B in the Mod+Harm phase specifically (safety intervention 8.3% under delusional framing vs 37.2% distress-only) and must not be quoted as a general result.** Delusion confirmation under delusional framing: <5% of turns for Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.5, 41% for Llama-3.3-70B, 53–61% for the three smaller open models; all models below 6% in distress-only. Measures response behavior, not clinical outcomes; only three delusion themes (no persecutory, somatic, or substance-induced); 16-turn cap far shorter than published case reports; judge shares an architecture with one evaluated model — **tested in Appendix H, where an alternative judge reproduces the crossover and the Del+Rec effect (OR=0.26), so the conclusions hold**. **Full text and all fourteen appendices inspected 19 October 2026; nothing in the previously unread portion contradicts any figure above.** The stated design does not yield 4,200 conversations and **the paper never reconciles this anywhere, appendices included** — the figure appears only in the introduction and discussion and no appendix reports a conversation total. ## F. Evidence gaps and sources to monitor

- Prospective population studies with exposed-user denominators. *Searched directly in the 19 October 2026 round and still open — the largest gap. Two near-misses that are not it: entry 80 has a 53,974-patient denominator but no ascertainment of chatbot exposure, and entry 36 has an exposed-user denominator but no clinical outcome and no independent verification. A lead suggesting the entry 76 group had funding for longitudinal follow-up of elevated-risk AI users was **checked against primary sources on 19 October 2026 and could not be verified** — NIH RePORTER (no such award to either author), ClinicalTrials.gov (no matching registration), the paper's own funding statement (a career award on early psychosis, not an AI cohort), and the institutional announcement (a longitudinal study of adolescents and digital risks broadly, never mentioning psychosis, with no funder named). It appears to be a conflation and **no entry has been created**. It remains marked unverified in [document 16](16-clinical-records-open-weight-models-and-trajectory-update.md) §7, which itemizes what was checked so the next round need not repeat it.*
- Multi-center clinical registries using standardized definitions. *Case strengthened by the 19 October 2026 round: entries 22 and 80 are two single-region screens in two countries using incompatible methods — AI-content classification versus product-name keywords — and cannot be pooled.*
- Full-transcript, version-aware case series. *Still open. Entry 80 is note-based and barred from publishing case detail; entry 82 proposes consented transcript donation as the route, which is a method proposal, not evidence.*
- Independent replications of vendor safety evaluations. *Still open. Recording entry 36's figures is not replication of them.*
- Controlled studies of sleep friction, anti-dependence design, and human handoff.
- Studies involving older adults, people with established psychosis histories, and non-Western cultures. *Partially addressed for adolescents (entries 78–79) and for young adults with elevated psychosis-risk symptoms (entry 76) in the 15 September 2026 round; both new human samples are US-only and neither covers ages above 25. Further addressed by entry 80 in the 19 October 2026 round, which adds a non-US health system and a population already in psychiatric care — but its median age is 28 (IQR 22–39), so older adults remain uncovered, and Denmark is not a non-Western population.*
- Long-term recovery and recurrence outcomes.
- Comparative results across therapy bots, voice agents, and local models. *General assistants versus companion bots were compared in entry 79 using simulated personas. Entry 81 adds the first quantified open-weight-versus-proprietary comparison on delusion confirmation. Voice agents and therapy bots remain effectively uncompared: entry 83 is a first-party feasibility report with no quantified safety results, and **its v2, inspected 19 October 2026, adds none** — a second version went by without the one thing that would have made it a safety result.*
- Validated adverse-event definitions and mandatory reporting rules. *Partly addressed in statute rather than in science by the 19 October 2026 docket review: California §22603 requires annual crisis-referral reporting to the Office of Suicide Prevention from 1 July 2027 and §21815 an Attorney General incident-reporting mechanism by 1 January 2028 (entries 48 and 84), and New York §1701 requires crisis-referral protocols (entry 49). **These create reporting duties, not validated definitions**, and none of them yields an adverse-event taxonomy or an exposed-user denominator.*
- Benchmark designs that score trajectories rather than endpoints, and that validate transcript-based scores against proximal human outcomes. *Added 19 October 2026 on the argument in entry 82; no empirical study meeting this description has been located.*
- **Regulatory and litigation status, re-verified against dockets rather than news.** *Opened as a standing monitoring item on 19 October 2026. The docket review recorded in [document 17](17-regulatory-docket-review-october-2026.md) covers eleven US jurisdictions, FDA, FTC and the EU AI Act, and leaves fourteen named questions unresolved — including Rhode Island's enacted status, three states' effective dates, whether an FDA final guidance or an FTC 6(b) staff report exists, and the posture of* Raine v. OpenAI, *for which no authoritative docket was reached. **A fifty-state census has never been performed, and dockets go stale between fetches: re-fetch before any product decision that turns on a case's posture.***
